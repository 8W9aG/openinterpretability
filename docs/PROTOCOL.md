# Audit protocol (v0.8)

This document defines what one Open Interpretability experiment is, who does what, and how a verdict is decided. The code implements it: the decision rule is `openinterp.agents.reviewer.evaluate_submission`, and its parameters are `openinterp.core.schemas.ReviewPolicy`. Every verdict stores the policy it was computed under, so any record can be re-checked with `openinterp reevaluate`.

## 1. Unit of analysis

One experiment tests **one hypothesis about one unit** in one model revision. A unit is a neuron pole or, since protocol 0.6, an attention head. A latent of a published sparse dictionary (a transcoder or SAE) on an MLP layer can be audited too. It is the learned-basis baseline for the native-neuron premise. Its experiments run under the same rule but are never counted with native units.

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Neuron | One coordinate of the MLP intermediate activation, i.e. the input to the down/output projection (`act(gate(x)) * up(x)` for gated MLPs). |
| Pole | Positive: `clamp(a, min=0)`. Negative: `clamp(a, max=0)`. The two poles are audited separately. |
| Attention head | One head's output z: its slice of the attention output projection's input (`o_proj`, or `c_proj` in GPT-2). Identified by `HeadIdentifier` (`kind = "attn_head"`, `L<layer>/H<idx>`). A head has no sign, so no poles. |
| Dictionary feature | One latent f of a sparse dictionary trained on a layer's MLP or on the residual stream after it. Identified by `FeatureIdentifier` (`kind = "dictionary_feature"`, `L<layer>/F<idx>` plus the dictionary's name, a preset in `harness/dictionaries.py`). The submission's `dictionary_profile` pins the weights (repository, commit, file) and how they are read. That covers the activation function and the input site. The input site is `resid_mid`, `mlp_in`, `mlp_in_normalized`, `mlp_out` for an MLP-output SAE, `resid_post` for a residual-stream SAE, or `mlp_hidden` for a `basis`. `resid_mid` is the residual stream before the MLP's norm. `mlp_in_normalized` is the norm's input standardised without its gain and bias, the TransformerLens `ln2.hook_normalized` that most public transcoders were trained on. `mlp_hidden` is the down projection's input, where native neurons live. A basis is the null-calibration control. Each latent is the positive part of one direction in the MLP's hidden space. With the identity basis the latents are exactly the native positive poles (`docs/analyses/null-calibration.md`). The profile also covers the output site: the MLP's output (after Gemma 2's post-MLP norm), or for a residual-stream SAE the residual stream it reads. Latents are non-negative, so no poles. |
| Strength | Non-negative, per token. Larger always means more active. For a neuron pole, the magnitude of the pole. For a dictionary feature, the latent's activation `a_f = act(W_enc[:, f] · x + b_enc[f])`. For a head, `\|W_O (z_t − z̄)\|`: the distance between the head's write to the residual stream at token t and its mean write over the scan. That is how far mean-ablating the head would move the residual stream there. z̄ is measured by the scan and stored in `head_profile.mean_output`, so every later measurement uses the same zero point and a record reproduces without its corpus. |
| Scale | The maximum strength seen during the discovery scan (`max_activation`), over the scan's corpus sample (§3, step 2). |
| Active | A prompt is active if its peak strength over its measured tokens is at least `activation_threshold × scale` (default 0.25). The measured tokens are every token, or every token but the first for a unit with a first-token floor. |
| First-token floor | Since protocol 0.8, a unit active at a text's first position on at least `max_active_share` (default 0.5) of the vocabulary. Activity there is measured against the line set by its scale without first tokens, and special tokens are left out. Attention there sees only the token itself, so this is a property of the weights. A text's first token has no context and is the attention sink. Such a unit sits above its line there whatever the token, so every probe would fire. For it, the first token is never measured. The scan leaves first tokens out of its scale, histogram and top-example ranking, and every peak below skips it. A one-token probe or counterfactual is then recorded as unmeasured (`measured = false`) and counts in no metric. The scan records the judgement in `first_token_floor`. A review of a record without one judges it against the record's scale. Every other unit is measured at every token, so a unit that detects *which* token opens a text can still be audited. |
| Default-on | A unit active on at least `max_active_share` (default 0.5) of its scan's tokens, read from the scan's histogram. At that density a probe of a few words almost always has one active token. So no hard negative can stay inactive, and the unit can't be audited as a detector. The scan stops on such a unit, as it does on one that never fires, and a review fails the `not_default_on` gate. The scan keeps the stop: in `wiki/draws/` for a random draw, and in `wiki/stops/` with a page for a chosen unit, which random-draw statistics don't read. In GPT-2 small most negative poles are default-on, because GELU's dip holds them near its minimum on most tokens (`docs/analyses/position-zero.md`). |
| Δ (counterfactual) | `(peak strength on base − peak strength on counterfactual) / scale`. |

## 2. Roles

| Role | Does | Must not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Proposer** | Selects the neuron, scans a corpus, writes the hypothesis, the mechanism claim and the minimal-pair counterfactuals. | Edit a submission after review. A revision is a new submission. |
| **Reviewer** | Writes adversarial probes from the hypothesis text and the top examples. Should be a different model from the proposer (see below). | See probe activations before the probe set is final. |
| **Harness** | Runs every measurement against the model weights and applies the decision rule. | Use LLM judgement anywhere in the verdict. |

Agents only write text: hypotheses and probes. Every number in a verdict comes from running the model.

**Reviewer isolation is enforced for the Claude Code reviewer.** A `PreToolUse` hook in `.claude/agents/neuron-reviewer.md` runs `openinterp/core/isolation.py` before each of the agent's tool calls. It denies reads, writes and searches of `wiki/experiments/`, `submissions/`, `logs/`, `build/` and `dist/`, and of `exchange/` except prompts (`*.prompt.md`) and probe sets (`*.probes.json`). If the hook fails, the call is denied. `Glob` is allowed, because it gives only file names. `openinterp usage` also checks the reviewer's transcript with the same rule. It writes each blocked path that the reviewer read to the usage file, and `review --probes` then refuses the probe set. Only paths in the checkout are blocked, and an API-key reviewer has no file tools.

**Reviewer independence is encouraged, not enforced.** A model probing its own hypothesis tends to share its blind spots, so a reviewer from a different model is stronger evidence. Since protocol 0.4 a same-model review is allowed: the record stores `independent_review = false` and the verdict is computed as usual (`require_independent_review` turns it into a gate, §4). In return, self-reviews are made visible and followed up:

- `verify` (alias `review`) warns when the reviewer model is the proposer's.
- Experiment, neuron, layer and home pages badge the verdict *self-reviewed*, wiki pages mark it, and `registry.parquet` has a `self_reviewed` column. The home page reports how many experiments and current verified claims are self-reviewed.
- `openinterp next` proposes an independent re-review of any unit whose latest experiment was self-reviewed, and a `replicate` of a VERIFIED unit that no other model has reviewed.

"Same model" means the same recorded model ID, so record the IDs the agents report.

## 3. Lifecycle

```text
select ─▶ scan ─▶ hypothesize ─▶ submit (frozen) ─▶ probe ─▶ measure ─▶ decide ─▶ record ─▶ publish
                       ▲                                                    │
                       └──────────── NEEDS_REVISION: new submission ◀───────┘
```

1. **Select.** Choose the unit and record how in `provenance.selection`. Agents without an assignment use `openinterp next` (skill `choose-next-experiment`). It explores uniformly random neurons with probability ε, in a model drawn from `[tool.openinterp] models` in `pyproject.toml`. The smallest model with less than `coverage_target` (80%) of its neurons verified gets most of these draws, and the others share the rest (`openinterp/planner_models.py`). A pole that the weights prove dead counts as verified. Otherwise it samples a follow-up on an existing neuron. Re-studying a neuron is valid, and null results count as data. The options are:
   - `random`: uniform over layers, then neurons, then poles (`openinterp audit --random --seed N`), or over layers, then heads (`--unit head --random`), or over a dictionary's layers, then its latents (`--dictionary <name> --random`). Only randomly selected units count towards registry-wide statistics such as "fraction of neurons with a verified explanation". Neurons, heads and dictionary features are never pooled. The baseline is the share VERIFIED among random latents against the share among random native neuron poles on the same model and layers. Each unit counts once, by its first experiment, so neither side gains from follow-ups. In a GELU MLP (GPT-2) the native arm is positive poles (`--random --pole positive`, uniform over layers, then neurons). The negative pole is GELU's dip, and it sits in the dip on most tokens. So a probe's peak nearly always clears the activity line, and no hard negative can pass. On the GPT-2 pilot, none of 6 random negative poles was verified (`docs/analyses/transcoder-baseline-pilot.md`). Since protocol 0.8 such poles are default-on (§1) and are not audited. 87% of GPT-2 small's negative poles are default-on. The test reported first is latents against positive poles. A random draw of a latent that never fires on the scan can't be audited. The scan of every live latent therefore records the share of its dictionary's latents that are dead on the same tokens (`dictionary_profile.dead_share`). The comparison reports it next to the shares VERIFIED. A random draw the scan stops on, dead or default-on, is kept as an outcome of random selection. `audit --random` writes it write-once to `wiki/draws/<slug>/<timestamp>.json` (`UnauditableDraw`, with its provenance). The build exports `data/draws.parquet`, and the comparison counts each arm's unauditable draws.
   - `attribution`: chosen because it ranked highly in attribution patching for some behaviour.
   - `manual`: anything else.
2. **Scan.** Run a sample of the corpus through the model and keep the top-k activating snippets and a histogram of all token strengths.
   - *Sample.* Since protocol 0.7 a scan reads a fixed-size, seeded sample of whole documents, not the whole corpus. So its cost and its scale don't grow with the corpus. Documents are visited in `random.Random(seed)` order, and each is taken if it fits in what is left of the token budget (the first always is). Tokens are counted with the model's tokenizer, without special tokens. The defaults are a budget of 200,000 tokens and seed 0 (`--max-tokens`, `--sample-seed`), so every audit of one corpus with one tokenizer reads the same documents. `provenance.corpus_sample` records the seed, budget, document and token counts and the SHA-256 of the sampled documents as JSONL. `--max-tokens 0` scans the whole corpus and records no sample, as every record before 0.7 did.
   - *Reading.* Documents are read whole, or as overlapping windows (`--window-tokens`, `--stride`) in which each token is counted once and top examples don't overlap. For a unit with a first-token floor (§1), first tokens are left out of the scale and histogram. Top examples are ranked by their peak after them. Record the model revision, dtype, corpus, corpus hash, sample, token count and window length and stride in `provenance`. A head scan first averages the head's output over every counted token (z̄), then measures strengths against it. It also records, for each top example, the tokens its peak attends to most. It records the head's previous-token, duplicate-token and induction scores too. These are mean attention weights on 16 sequences of 32 uniformly random tokens repeated twice (Olsson et al., 2022). These are evidence for the proposer, not gates. Heads have no vocabulary lens (a head writes a subspace, not one direction) and no partner search. A dictionary-feature scan reads the dictionary's input in the same pass and also measures how well the whole dictionary fits there. It records the fraction of its output site's variance it reconstructs, its mean L0 and the latent's density (`dictionary_profile`). The fraction leaves out each piece's first token, an attention sink the dictionaries weren't trained on. These are reported, not gated, but a dictionary that fits badly is a weak baseline. A feature's vocabulary lens reads its decoder row. It has no partner search or input attribution.
3. **Hypothesize.** The proposer states:
   - a natural-language explanation precise enough that a third party can label new text as "predicted active" or "predicted inactive".
   - the mechanism: **retrospective** (reacts to tokens already read) or **lookahead** (prepares specific upcoming tokens).
   - at least three minimal-pair counterfactuals with predicted Δ, including at least one control pair (predicted Δ ≈ 0).
4. **Submit.** The submission is frozen from here on. Changing anything other than provenance metadata produces a new submission.
5. **Probe.** The reviewer writes 10–20 probes, of at least two tokens each for a unit with a first-token floor. It labels each with what the *hypothesis* predicts, not with what the reviewer expects the neuron to do:
   - **hard negatives** (about two thirds): near-misses that share surface features with the activating examples but which the hypothesis says should not fire.
   - **positive holdouts** (at least four): new contexts, including paraphrases of the claimed feature, that the hypothesis says should fire.
6. **Measure.** The harness runs every probe and counterfactual, and computes the causal metrics. It also measures the lookahead profile: the KL between clean and ablated next-token distributions, and how concentrated the change is. A pole is ablated by zeroing it. A head is ablated by replacing its output with z̄ (mean ablation), the change its strength measures. A dictionary feature is ablated by subtracting its write `a_f · W_dec[f]` from its output site. That leaves the dictionary's reconstruction error and every other latent in place.
7. **Decide.** Apply the rule in §4.
8. **Record.** Every completed experiment, whatever its verdict, gets a write-once page in `wiki/experiments/<model>/L<layer>/N<idx>-<pole>/<timestamp>.md` (`…/L<layer>/H<idx>/` for a head, `…/L<layer>/<dictionary>/F<idx>/` for a dictionary feature). The page holds the hypothesis, evidence, gate results, failed probes and next steps derived from the failed gates. `verify` and `reevaluate` write it automatically, next to the experiment's write-once record `<timestamp>.json`. Re-evaluations, revisions and re-reviews are new experiments: nothing is ever overwritten. A revision names the experiment it replaces in its submission's `supersedes` field (`audit --supersedes <record>`), so the history reads as a chain. The page's Discussion section is for free-form notes.
9. **Publish.** Every experiment is published, whatever its verdict. The site ([open-interpretability.com](https://open-interpretability.com/)) and `registry.parquet` are built from the archived `<id>.json` records, the registry's only source of truth. A neuron pole or head may have any number of experiments, and its standing (see *Standing* below) is derived from them, never stored. Pages follow Model → Layer → Neuron (`N<idx>.html`), head (`H<idx>.html`) or dictionary feature (`<dictionary>/F<idx>.html`) → Experiments, and each unit's experiments are ordered by **conclusiveness**:
   - With Beta(1, 1) priors on specificity and sensitivity, P(useful) = P(balanced accuracy > 0.6).
   - Conclusiveness is |2·P(useful) − 1|, so a well-probed rejection ranks as high as a well-probed verification, and thinly-probed results rank low whatever their verdict.

   **Standing.** A unit's status is not simply its newest experiment's, or a weaker or self-reviewed rerun could overturn an independent VERIFIED. It is derived in four steps (`builder.standing.pole_standing`):
   1. *Newest hypothesis.* Only experiments on the unit's newest submission count. A new submission replaces older ones (a revision names them in `supersedes`), and their verdicts were about a different claim. Re-reviews of one submission share everything but provenance.
   2. *One reading per probe set.* A re-measurement of a probe set (e.g. `reevaluate` under a newer protocol) replaces that set's earlier reading.
   3. *Independent over self-review.* If any reading came from a reviewer model other than the proposer's, self-reviews are outweighed.
   4. *Agreement.* If the readings left agree, that is the status. If one is VERIFIED and another is not, the status is **CONTESTED**, and the unit is not counted as verified. If none is VERIFIED, the newest reading's status stands: NEEDS_REVISION against REJECTED is not a disagreement about whether the claim holds.

   Unit pages show which reviews the status rests on, and mark every other experiment as re-measured, outweighed or an earlier hypothesis. The home page lists contested units and reports **reviewer agreement**. It takes the hypotheses reviewed with more than one probe set, self-reviews included, each set read by its newest experiment. It reports how many got the same verdict, VERIFIED or not, from every review, and the agreement rate over pairs of reviews. `registry.parquet` has `unit_status` and `counts_toward_status`.

## 4. Decision rule

Probes are not independent: one reviewer writes them all, often as variations on a theme. Near-duplicate probes (word-set Jaccard ≥ `probe_cluster_threshold`, default 0.5) are therefore clustered, and each cluster counts as **one observation**, scored by its mean pass rate. Every count and rate below uses these effective counts.

Probe accuracy is reported as **balanced accuracy**, the mean of:

- **specificity**: the cluster-weighted fraction of hard negatives that stayed inactive.
- **sensitivity**: the cluster-weighted fraction of positive holdouts that fired.

Balanced accuracy is the `confidence_score`. Using it means many easy negatives cannot hide failed generalisations.

**Confound control.** The scan records a confound profile of the pole. It holds the variance explained by token identity and by position, and the correlation with token frequency. It also holds the tokens and positions with the highest mean strength. On the reviewer's probes, the hypothesis is compared with three trivial baselines, each scored by the same cluster-weighted balanced accuracy:

- *token identity*: active if any measured token is a trigger token.
- *position*: active at a high-activation measured position.
- *never active*.

A hypothesis that can't at least match the best of these adds nothing beyond "fires on token X".

A verdict is **VERIFIED** only if every gate passes:

<!-- Defaults quote ReviewPolicy in core/schemas.py, and tests/test_single_source.py checks them. -->

| Gate | Default | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `enough_hard_negatives` | ≥ 6 clusters | Specificity is meaningless on a handful of independent probes. |
| `enough_holdouts` | ≥ 3 clusters | The same applies to sensitivity. |
| `specificity` | ≥ 0.80 | The hypothesis is not too broad. |
| `sensitivity` | ≥ 0.60 | The hypothesis generalises beyond its own examples. |
| `counterfactual_reproduction` | ≥ 0.75 of pairs | The proposer's predicted Δ reproduce within `max(0.10, 0.50 × |predicted|)`, with the right sign. |
| `lookahead_consistent` | — | The measured mechanism matches the claim. |
| `independent_review` | off (`require_independent_review`) | The reviewer model differs from the proposer model. Always recorded, and gated only when the policy requires it. |
| `beats_confound_baseline` | ≥ best baseline | The hypothesis explains the probes at least as well as token identity, position or "never active". It fails when the submission has no confound profile. |
| `not_default_on` | active share < 0.50 | The unit is not default-on (§1), so a hard negative can stay inactive at all. It passes when the submission has no histogram. |

If the probe set is adequate (both count gates pass) and balanced accuracy is below **0.60**, the verdict is **REJECTED**. The hypothesis then predicts the neuron barely better than chance. Every other outcome is **NEEDS_REVISION**, and the failed gates are listed in `verdict.notes`.

### What is not evidence (yet)

- **The proposer's counterfactuals.** The proposer can measure these before submitting, so a match is a reproduction check (it catches measurement or transcription errors) and not a successful prediction. The predictive test is the reviewer's probes, which are written without seeing any activations.
- **Covariance with other neurons.** Pole strengths are sparse and heavy-tailed, so a global correlation mostly measures agreement on "nothing is happening". The scan therefore finds partner neuron *poles* by co-activation lift: P(partner active | target active) ÷ P(partner active).
  - *Target active* uses the protocol threshold. *Partner active* means above the partner pole's own 99th percentile.
  - Partners are ranked by the 95% Wilson lower bound of the lift, so a few chance coincidences don't count.
  - Each partner also records Spearman's ρ of the two pole strengths, on a fixed-seed reservoir sample with ties averaged. For reference, it also records Pearson's r with the signed activation.
  - The reviewer ablates the pole with up to 3 partners (lift lower bound ≥ 2 on ≥ 5 co-active tokens, or |ρ| ≥ 0.2). It reports the KL for the pole alone, the partners alone and all together.
  - The *redundancy ratio* is (KL together − KL partners) / KL alone. It exceeds 1 when partners mask the pole's effect, and is near 0 when the pole's effect overlaps with theirs.

  This is reported, not gated.
- **Vocabulary lens.** Each submission records the pole's output direction read through the logit lens and the **Jacobian lens** (Gurnee et al., 2026). The Jacobian lens transports the direction with the context-averaged Jacobian E[∂h_final,t′/∂h_ℓ,t] over t′ ≥ t, estimated from one perturbed forward pass per prompt and sign. The record includes:
  - both top-10 lists, with each token's z-score over the whole vocabulary.
  - their overlap and cos(w, J·w).
  - the kurtosis of the vocabulary scores (≈ 3 means no specific token set).

  On Qwen2.5-0.5B the lenses disagree completely up to the middle layers and converge near the output, so mechanism claims should rest on the Jacobian lens. It is reported, not gated.
- **Patchscope.** Each submission on a neuron pole or dictionary feature records the model's own description of the unit's output direction. That direction is a pole's signed `w_out`, or a latent's decoder row. It uses a Patchscope (Ghandeharioun et al., 2024) in the spirit of SelfIE (Chen et al., 2024). The direction replaces the residual stream after a layer's block at the placeholder `?` of an inspection prompt. It is scaled to 1, 2 and 4 times the norm of the residual it replaces. The model then continues greedily for up to 12 tokens.
  - *Prompts.* `category` is a few-shot list (`banana: fruit`, `hammer: tool`, `violin: music`, `senator: politics`, then `?:`). `meaning` is `The meaning of the word "?" is`.
  - *Layers.* The unit's own layer, and layer 2 when that is earlier.
  - *Control.* The same with a fixed random direction (seed 0). Text the two share comes from the prompt, not the unit.

  The readout is taken after the proposer has answered, and no prompt shows it. So it is a blind, example-free description that can later serve as a proposer baseline. A proposer revising an earlier experiment can still read it on that experiment's page. On Qwen2.5-0.5B, `category` at layer 2 named the concept of several VERIFIED poles. It gave *crop* for L14/N2611+, *newspaper* for L5/N4579−, *official* for L3/N3738− and *culture* for L5/N2131−. For those poles the control gave *animal*, *emotion* or *period*. Injected at their own layer, poles from layer 17 up mostly read back the control's text. It costs about 5–8 s per unit on CPU and is reported, not gated. Heads write a subspace, not one direction, and are not read.

  *As a proposer baseline* (`openinterp patchscope-baseline`, results in `docs/analyses/patchscope-baseline.md`), each unit's readout becomes one explanation by a fixed rule. The rule takes the `category` reading at the lowest layer read, at ×2, and writes `Fires on tokens related to "<reading>".` as the explanation. It is scored on the held-out windows the agent's explanation was scored on. Records store those windows with their labels, so the two are paired and nothing is re-measured. Detection and fuzzing pair only experiments judged by the same model. Claude Haiku 5.5 judged 44 held-out Qwen2.5-0.5B experiments. On those, the baseline scored at chance (detection 0.51, fuzzing 0.52) against the agent's 0.60 and 0.62. It matched or beat the agent on 20% of experiments for detection (sign test p = 1e-8) and 30% for fuzzing (p = 2e-4). Embedding AUROC was 0.53 against 0.55 over 89 experiments (p = 0.24). The readings are mostly generic: 38 distinct over 99 experiments, led by *country*, *noun* and *animal*. Judges read grammatical readings like *noun* inconsistently, as the word or as the part of speech.
- **Neuronpedia baseline** (`openinterp neuronpedia-baseline`, results in `docs/analyses/neuronpedia-baseline.md`). This applies to a dictionary latent that [Neuronpedia](https://www.neuronpedia.org) hosts (today the GPT-2 transcoders, `tres-dc`). The rule, fixed before scoring, takes the latent's first machine-written Neuronpedia explanation word for word. Human-written ones are left out. It is scored on the record's own held-out windows, as the Patchscope baseline is, and the row credits Neuronpedia and the model that wrote it. Explanations are read once from Neuronpedia's public API and cached. Nothing is sent to Neuronpedia. Claude Haiku 5.5 judged 36 GPT-2 experiments, where Neuronpedia's gpt-4o-mini explanations scored detection 0.74 against the agent's 0.78 (sign test p = 0.12). On 15 experiments with fuzzing windows, they scored 0.63 against 0.67 (p = 1). Embedding AUROC was 0.72 against 0.75 over 30 experiments (p = 0.36). *Through the review* (`openinterp neuronpedia-review`, results in `docs/analyses/neuronpedia-review.md`), each latent's newest submission is copied with Neuronpedia's explanation as the hypothesis. The copy claims no activating tokens and a retrospective mechanism, so no claim of the agent's reaches the reviewer. A `neuron-reviewer` writes probes for the copy, and the harness measures them. Neuronpedia makes no counterfactual or mechanism claims, so both sides are judged only on the gates that the probes decide. The reviews are not registry records. Over 36 GPT-2 latents, with Claude Sonnet 5.5 as reviewer, Neuronpedia's explanations passed every probe gate on 11 and the agents' on 35. Balanced accuracy was 0.68 against 0.95 (sign test p = 4e-8), and sensitivity 0.46 against 0.92. The agents' side is favoured: it is each unit's newest experiment, often a revision.
- **Probe comparison** (`openinterp probe-compare`, results in `docs/analyses/probe-comparison.md`). This applies to a VERIFIED neuron pole whose concept a rule over characters can mark. The rule is a regular expression, or human annotations written as spans by `openinterp concepts`, never the model. Linear probes are trained on labelled tokens of one corpus and tested on another. They are logistic and mass-mean probes (Marks & Tegmark, 2023) on the layer's MLP hidden activations and on the residual stream after the layer. There are also k-sparse logistic probes over the hidden layer's neurons (Gurnee et al., 2023). The report gives the pole's own held-out accuracy, at a fitted threshold and at the protocol's line. It gives the cosine between each probe's direction and the pole's (its signed basis vector, or its signed `w_out` on the residual). It also gives the neuron's rank by class separation, and the smallest k that comes within 0.02 of the dense probe. It shows whether the concept is localised in the neuron or only partly carried by it. Each text window's first token is left out. Records are write-once, so it is not stored in them. It is reported, not gated.
- **Steering samples.** Each submission on a neuron pole or dictionary feature records five neutral prompts (`openinterp/harness/steering.py`), each continued greedily for up to 24 tokens. The continuations run under each of five interventions, applied at every position:
  - *baseline*: none.
  - *ablated*: the unit's own ablation (the pole zeroed, or the latent's write removed).
  - *clamped ×1, ×2, ×4*: a neuron's activation set to the pole's signed top activation in the scan (`max_activation`) times the multiple. A latent's activation is set to that value.

  Coherent drift at ×1–×2 says what the unit makes the model write (Turner et al., 2023, and Goodfire, 2026). Drift only at ×4 is off-distribution. No effect is common and does not refute a detection claim. The samples are taken after the proposer has answered and no prompt shows them. A steering prediction needs a pre-registered format before it can be gated. It is reported, not gated, and heads, which have no single "high" setting, are not steered.
- **Wiring.** Each unit records the units it connects to most directly along the residual stream (`openinterp/harness/wiring.py`, `wiring_activations.py`), the 10 strongest of each kind, read two ways. A neuron pole reads its MLP input (`gate` and `up` for a gated MLP, else `in`) and writes `w_out`, negated for the negative pole. A dictionary feature reads its site with its encoder row and writes its decoder row. A head reads through Q, K and V and writes the subspace `W_O`, so its own layer's MLP is downstream of it.
  - *weights* (`NeuronAuditSubmission.wiring`): virtual weights (Elhage et al., 2021). Upstream are earlier neurons' `w_out` and earlier heads' OV circuits, against the unit's read. Downstream are later neurons' reads, and later heads' Q, K and V, of the unit's write. Each number is signed when one vector reads another, and a norm otherwise. Each norm before a read is folded in as its gain, plus centring for LayerNorm. Its per-token scale is dropped, so weights rank units but have no absolute scale. Grouped-query heads share their key and value reads. It takes under a second and is read after the proposer has answered.
  - *activations* (`VerificationVerdict.wiring`): attribution patching (Syed et al., 2023) at the unit's peak, averaged over the review's should-fire prompts that reach the activation threshold. Those prompts are the counterfactuals' base prompts and the holdouts.
    - Upstream is `Σ_s write_s · ∂strength/∂x_s`, the first-order change in strength from removing one unit's direct write at every position.
    - A downstream neuron gets the exact change in its activation at the peak when the unit's write is removed from its input alone.
    - A downstream head gets `|W_O Δz|` at the peak when the unit's write is removed from its Q, K or V input alone. This is done one channel at a time, with everything else held fixed.
    - It takes about 8 s for a Qwen2.5-0.5B neuron on CPU, most of it on downstream heads.

  Both readings are direct effects only, not paths through other units, and no prompt shows them. Archived records are write-once, so `openinterp wiring-backfill` takes both readings for records that predate wiring, the same way, at each record's model revision and dtype. It writes them to `docs/analyses/wiring.jsonl`, which `openinterp build` shows, marked as backfilled. Site pages link each edge to the unit's page when the registry has one. Each model's *circuits* page joins units that both have pages: edges are followed from unit to unit into connected groups, laid out by layer. A *hub* is a unit wired to at least 5% of the model's units with pages (and at least 10). It would join most of them into one star, so its edges don't join circuits. The page lists hubs and the units wired to each separately. A unit's wiring lists only its top connections, so a missing edge is not evidence of no connection. Reported, not gated.
- **Dictionary latents.** `openinterp crossref` takes each neuron pole whose model has a dictionary preset at its layer (`openinterp/harness/dictionaries.py`). It lists the 5 latents whose decoder row best aligns with the pole's write. For a dictionary reading the MLP's input, it also lists the 5 whose encoder best aligns with the neuron's read (`openinterp/harness/crossref.py`). Cosines are taken in residual-stream coordinates with norms folded in as for wiring, and are oriented. A latent fires non-negatively, so it matches a pole only along the pole's own write (`±w_out`). It matches a read along `in` or `up` with the pole's sign, and along `gate` positively. Each side records *chance*, the 99th percentile of the best cosine a random direction reaches. It also records the share of the layer's neurons whose best match is weaker. Weights only, so it holds for every experiment on the pole: rows go to `docs/analyses/dictionary-alignment.jsonl`, keyed by pole and preset, with the dictionary's file pinned. They name each latent by index, never copy its description. Neuron pages list them, linked to the latent's page here if it was audited. Otherwise they link to Neuronpedia, if the preset names a Neuronpedia source checked to index the same latents. `openinterp aligned-latents <slug>` prints them with descriptions, and `openinterp review --dictionary-latents` shows the reviewer the above-chance ones that have a description, to probe where they disagree with the hypothesis. That flag is off by default, so a reviewer's prompt doesn't depend on a third-party service. Reported, not gated.
- **Prior art** (`openinterp/prior_art.py`). After the proposer answers, `audit` looks up what others have published about the unit. Today the only source is [Neuronpedia](https://www.neuronpedia.org), through the cached lookup of `openinterp/crossref.py`. A dictionary latent that Neuronpedia hosts gets its own first machine-written explanation. A neuron pole gets the explanations of the latents aligned with it. They come from each dictionary that Neuronpedia hosts at its layer, from `openinterp crossref` or aligned on the spot. Up to 3 latents above chance count on each side, by |cosine|. The submission records each one with its relation, cosine, explainer and link. The proposer never sees prior art. The reviewer always sees it, as a source of probes where it disagrees with the hypothesis, and the unit page shows it. A failed lookup is left out, and `$OPENINTERP_PRIOR_ART=0` turns the readout off. Reported, not gated.
- **Sister neurons.** `openinterp sisters` matches each VERIFIED neuron pole with the poles of another model (`openinterp/harness/sisters.py`). By default it compares every ordered pair of the configured models (`[tool.openinterp] models`), and each model with its larger siblings (`SIBLINGS`, Qwen2.5-0.5B → Qwen2.5-1.5B). Both models read the corpus (`corpus.jsonl`) a whole document per forward pass, cut at the model's context. They are compared at *shared boundaries*: character offsets where a token ends in both tokenizations (the last token, where several end at one character). Both models are causal, so at a shared boundary each has read exactly the same text, whatever its tokens. Models with one tokenizer share every boundary. Each row records its *coverage*, the share of the source model's tokens that paired. Each text's first token is left out, as many units take extreme values there. Each pole's strength is correlated (Pearson, over all paired positions) with every pole of every layer of the other model. Its 5 best matches are kept. A second pass correlates each match with every pole of the first model. A match whose own best partner is the pole is a *mutual* best match (Gurnee et al., 2024). *Rotated* is the best correlation the pole reaches with any unit of a Haar-random rotation of its top match's layer, seeded by model and layer. That gives as many candidates, built from the same activations, but not the other model's own neurons. A mutual best match above it is a *sister*. Rows go to `docs/analyses/sister-neurons.jsonl`, one per pole and other model, with both models' revisions and the corpus's SHA-256. Neuron pages list each pole's matches, and a matched pole's page lists the poles that matched it, linked where the registry has a page. Each model's page draws the graph with every other model it was compared with (`openinterp/builder/sister_graph.py`). It places both models' poles at their relative depth (layer over the deepest layer). Each VERIFIED pole is joined to its best match from either side's search, darker the higher the correlation, with the sisters in colour. A sister is a candidate for the same feature, not a test that the explanation carries over. `openinterp audit` and `openinterp review` show their agents a pole's linked poles that this registry has studied, with each one's standing and explanation. The linked poles are its sisters either way, and any other match with r of at least 0.5 (`MIN_PROMPT_PEARSON`). Weaker links mostly join unrelated features. The proposer is told to treat them as leads. The reviewer is told to probe where an explanation differs from the hypothesis. `--no-sisters` leaves them out.
  - *Sister study.* Whether a sister represents the same concept is an experiment. `openinterp audit --sister-of <VERIFIED record>` audits the sister with that record's explanation as its hypothesis. The hypothesis is recorded word for word whatever the proposer drafts (`sister_of` names the experiment). The proposer picks the activating tokens, mechanism and counterfactuals the explanation predicts for the sister, and the review is the usual one. VERIFIED says the explanation carries over. Any other verdict says where it doesn't. `openinterp next` offers `sister` draws in configured models only, so CI can reproduce the record. Any pole of another model can be drawn against a VERIFIED pole, but the draws lean heavily towards its sister. The sister has base weight 10, the highest, because the explanation is ready-made and the sister is its likeliest carrier. Each other unstudied match gets `LINK_WEIGHT` (0.05) × r of that. One draw per model picks a random pole no search matched (`UNMATCHED_WEIGHT`, 0.01 of it), the control for the matched draws. A sister study's unit may be chosen at random, but its hypothesis is borrowed, so statistics over random first audits leave sister studies out. Neuron pages give each tested pair's verdict, and the study's experiment links to the record it tested. Results: `docs/analyses/sister-neurons.md`. Reported, not gated.
- **The other pole.** The scan of a neuron pole reads the whole signed activation. So it also records the neuron's other pole at no extra cost: its 5 strongest non-overlapping examples and its histogram over the same tokens (`opposite_pole`). They are left out if that pole never fires. The neuron page shows both poles' top examples side by side, since one pole often encodes the contrast the other draws. Neither prompt shows them. Reported, not gated.
- **Label confounds.** A scan (of a neuron pole, a head or a dictionary latent) can label every counted token from a label set. It records what each set explains of the unit's activation (`ConfoundProfile.labels`, `openinterp/harness/annotations.py`). A tagger labels character spans of a document, and the scan aligns them to the model's tokens once per piece through the tokenizer's character offsets. Special tokens, characters no span covers and windows whose text can't be found in their document stay unlabelled and are left out.
  - *R².* Variance explained by a per-label mean, as `token_r2` is for token identity.
  - *Mutual information.* The mutual information between strength and label, in bits. Strengths are sparse and heavy-tailed, so the estimate is binned: zero is a bin of its own and positive strengths fall in 8 equal-count bins. Each entropy has the Miller–Madow correction, but small scans (a few hundred tokens) still read a little high.
  - *Per label.* The 50 most frequent labels, each with its token count, mean strength and number of tokens at or above the protocol's activation line.
  - *Top examples.* Each top example records its peak token's label in each set (`ActivatingExample.peak_labels`). A document label is its document's.

  The label sets are `language`, from a corpus's `lang` field, and `source`, from a blended corpus's `source` field: the preset each document was sampled from (`openinterp corpus --dataset mixed`). `openinterp corpus --dataset wikipedia-multilingual` writes it, taken exactly from each document's Wikipedia subset. For other datasets, `openinterp corpus --detect-lang` detects it per document with fastText's `lid.176` (`openinterp/langid.py`). From `mixed-v7`, `mixed` detects it in every preset except those two, so English web text, papers and chat record `en` too. It labels a document only at confidence ≥ 0.8, so most code and markup stay unlabelled. `docs/analyses/language-detection.md` explains the choice. The corpus's source sidecar records the detector. A corpus without the field records neither set, which includes the registry's scan corpus.

  Every scan since this version also records `personal data`, `toxicity`, `hate speech`, `names` and `postal addresses`, found in the text itself (`openinterp/sensitive.py`, `openinterp/toxicity.py`, `openinterp/names.py`). `provenance.label_detector` pins the packages, suffix list and models. Every token has a label, `none` when no match covers it, because a token with no label would leave both measures. `personal data` labels emails, phone numbers, payment cards, IBANs, US SSNs and ITINs, UK NHS numbers, crypto wallets and public IP addresses, by kind. A phone number has at least 10 digits, or 7 to 12 just after a phone word such as "tel" or "contact". `toxicity` and `hate speech` are models' guesses, per sentence of at least 2 words, so all of a sentence's tokens take its label. `toxicity` (`unitary/unbiased-toxic-roberta`) labels `threat`, `identity_attack`, `sexual_explicit`, `obscene`, `insult`, or `toxicity` when none of those passes. `hate speech` (`cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-hate-multiclass-latest`) labels `racism`, `sexism`, `religion`, `sexual_orientation` or `disability`. On HateCheck, which it did not train on, the second model found 40% of hateful cases, and called 9% of non-hateful cases hate. Both read English. `names` labels people's names found by spaCy's `en_core_web_trf`. It is a model's guess and mostly matches public and fictional people, so nothing removes text because of it. `postal addresses` labels street lines, PO boxes, US city-state-ZIP and UK postcodes, by pattern. It finds only those formats and cannot tell a home from a business, and nothing removes text because of it. Like all confounds, these are never gated, so they cannot change a verdict. Corpora are never screened, and scans, agents and records read the text unchanged. Only the site removes personal data and masks offensive examples, and only in what it publishes (`openinterp/builder/pii.py`). The Parquet export has `personal_data_*`, `offensive_words_*`, `names_*` and `postal_addresses_*` columns, to find such units across the registry.

  A corpus can also carry `code_language`, each code document's programming language, taken exactly from the dataset (`openinterp corpus --dataset github-code`, or `mixed` from `mixed-v2`). For other datasets, `openinterp corpus --detect-code` detects it per document with Google's Magika (`openinterp/codeid.py`). From `mixed-v7`, `mixed` detects it in every preset except `github-code`, so the Pile's code and code in chat answers record theirs. It labels a document only at confidence ≥ 0.8, and only with a github-code language, so prose and data files stay unlabelled. A detected label covers the whole document, so a Stack Exchange question labelled Python is parsed as Python, prose and all. `docs/analyses/code-detection.md` explains the choice. The corpus's source sidecar records the detector. It gives the label set `programming language`. A scan of such a corpus also parses each document in `GRAMMARS` (`openinterp/syntax.py`) with tree-sitter. It records five more label sets: `syntax node`, `bracket depth`, `round depth` (round brackets only), `indent depth` and `syntax construct`. `syntax node` is identifier, keyword, string, comment, number, operator, bracket, punctuation, whitespace or other. `syntax construct` is the innermost function, type definition, parameter list, call's arguments, import, loop or conditional, or `top level`. A string or a comment counts as one node, so a bracket inside it does not change the depth. The labels sort each grammar's node names by pattern, so they are approximate. Tokens of other documents stay unlabelled, and `provenance.label_detector` pins the parser and grammar versions. A corpus without the field records none of these, which includes the registry's scan corpus. Parsing a document that the corpus cut adds the closing brackets it lacks, and labels only the original text. `papers/syntax-positive-controls/README.md` tests these labels on GPT-2.

  The proposer sees each label set's R², mutual information and active tokens per label, and each top example's language. It sees `personal data`, `toxicity`, `hate speech` and `postal addresses` only when a match has active tokens, and a top example's match label only when it isn't `none`. It never sees `names`, because most units have an active token in some name. The reviewer sees each example's language. It is asked for positive holdouts translated into other languages when a hypothesis claims a feature whatever the language. It is asked for hard negatives in another language of the same script when the hypothesis names one. When a top example has syntax labels, the reviewer is asked for hard negatives that keep the token and change its syntactic role. An example is `return` as a keyword, in a string or in a comment. It is also asked for positive holdouts in another programming language when a hypothesis claims a feature whatever the language. The site lists each corpus with its language mix: per model, its largest scan's labelled tokens by language. Reported, not gated.
- **WordNet categories of the peak words** (`openinterp/wordnet.py`). A top example's peak word is the word that holds its peak token. Each peak word of at least 3 letters is looked up in WordNet 3.0 (through NLTK) as a noun. The lookup uses its first 3 senses and instance hypernyms ("Paris" is under "city"). A category is a synset that at least 3 peak words fall under, at depth 3 or more. A shallower synset is kept only when it covers words that no deeper one covers, so each is the lowest shared hypernym of its words. The proposer sees up to 4 categories, each with its depth, its words and the words outside it ("body part: 8 of 10"). The reviewer sees probe material for the largest one. Other hyponyms that are not peak words give positive holdouts, and sibling categories give hard negatives. Other senses of the peak words give same-word hard negatives. There is no word-sense disambiguation, and names, slang, code and other languages are poorly covered. Nothing is stored in the record: both notes are computed from the top examples when a prompt is written. Reported, not gated.
  *Specificity check* (`openinterp/harness/specificity.py`, `submission.wordnet_specificity`). After the proposer answers, the unit is measured on words of one WordNet category and on words of its sibling categories. This tells a "cities" unit from a "places" unit. The category is the synset that the proposer names in `wordnet_synset`, else the largest category of the peak words. The words are up to 40 hyponyms of the category, most frequent first, against up to 40 words that its sibling synsets give in turn. Peak words are left out of both lists. Each word sits in 3 neutral carrier sentences. Its strength in one sentence is the peak over the tokens that overlap the word. Its recorded strength is the mean over the sentences. The record gives each word's strength and the share of each list at or above the activation line. It also gives the AUROC of category words against sibling words. It is skipped when either list has fewer than 5 words. A word out of context is a weak test for a unit that needs context. So a low share active is not evidence against a hypothesis. Neither agent sees it. Reported, not gated.
- **Input attribution.** For a neuron pole, the scan takes the signed activation at the peak of each of the first 5 top examples. It attributes it to every token up to the peak. It uses integrated gradients over the input token embeddings (Sundararajan et al., 2017). The baseline replaces every token but the first with the pad token. The first token stays because it is the attention sink: replacing it sends the path off-distribution and the integral does not converge. Each attribution is a share of the gap between the example and the baseline, so the shares sum to 1 when the integral has converged.
  - *Convergence.* The steps double from 16 up to 64 until the shares sum to within 0.1 of 1. Only converged examples count towards the summary numbers.
  - *Distance.* The share of |attribution| on the peak token, the token before, and tokens 2–4, 5–16, 17–64 and 65+ back, with the weighted mean distance. This separates "fires on the current token" from "fires because of earlier context" (compare `token_r2`).
  - *Ablation check.* The peak, the 4 tokens before it and the 5 most attributed tokens are each replaced alone with the pad token and re-measured. The record gives Spearman's ρ between attribution and this effect.

  The proposer and the reviewer see the most attributed tokens of each example, with both numbers, so probes can vary the context that matters. On Qwen2.5-0.5B, L4/N1889+ converges on all 5 examples at 16 steps: 47% of its attribution is on the peak token and ρ = 0.76. In layers 11–20 the integral converges on 1 to 3 of 5 examples within 64 steps. There, most attribution often sits more than 16 tokens back, spread so thinly that replacing any one far token changes almost nothing. Replacing one of the few tokens just before the peak often removes it completely. It costs 6 s (layer 4) to about 2 min (layer 20) per neuron on CPU. It is reported, not gated, and heads are not attributed yet.
- **Causal metrics.** Sufficiency (clean → corrupted patch) and necessity (corrupted → clean patch) are measured on each minimal pair. Downstream MLP outputs are frozen so that they cannot compensate (the hydra effect). They are recorded, not gated.
  - *Where.* The pole is patched at every position and scored at the position where its strength differs most between the two prompts. When the prompts differ in token length, only the final position can be patched and scored.
  - *What.* The target favours no particular neuron. A lookahead claim is scored on its claimed upcoming tokens: their mean logit minus the vocabulary mean (`claimed_tokens`). Anything else is scored on the KL between the unpatched and patched next-token distributions (`next_token_kl`).
  - *Compared with what.* The same patch is applied, one neuron at a time, to the 32 same-layer neurons (`causal_null_neurons`) whose strength changes most similarly between the pair's prompts. A head is patched by swapping its output z and ranked against the other heads of its layer (at most `causal_null_neurons`). The change matched is each head's change in write, |W_O,h (z_base − z_cf)|, which needs no head's z̄. With a dozen or so heads per layer the percentile is coarse. A dictionary feature is patched by adding `(a_f,source − a_f,target) · W_dec[f]` to its output site. It is ranked against the 32 latents of the same dictionary whose write changes, |Δa| · ‖W_dec[f]‖, are most similar. Each score is the median over pairs of the pole's |effect| percentile among theirs. 0.5 means "no more influential than a neuron that changes as much". 1.0 means it beats all of them. The raw median effects are recorded too. So is each pair's effect and percentile in both directions, with the matched units' median and largest |effect| (`causal_metrics.pairs`).
  - *Which pairs.* Control pairs, where the pole moves by at most `delta_abs_tolerance × scale`, carry no causal signal and are skipped. With none left, no causal metrics are recorded.

  Two simpler scores read wrongly on Qwen2.5-0.5B. The fraction of the whole clean/corrupted gap restored (protocol ≤ 0.4, on the top-1 logit) reads ≈ 0 for any single neuron. The gap sums every component's contribution, and the rest of the model often moves the other way. A percentile against *random* neurons reads ≈ 1 for every neuron, because minimal pairs are built to change the audited neuron and most others barely change.

  The `next_token_kl` target shows that a neuron matters, not that it matters *for the reason the hypothesis gives*. A causal test of a retrospective claim would need the hypothesis to name the behaviour it affects. Gating on these scores needs a pre-registered prediction format first (see TODO).
- **Explanation scores on held-out text** (Delphi, Paulo et al., 2025), from `openinterp score`. Every review records them: `review` takes them with `--explanation-scores`, or measures them itself in one-step mode, and refuses to post without them. `reevaluate` makes no LLM calls, so it attaches them only when given. They test the explanation on text nobody chose, where probes test it on text the reviewer chose.
  - *Windows.* The corpus snippets that are not the scan's top examples, so the proposer never saw them, are cut into 32-token windows. They come from the whole corpus, not just the scan's sample. Each window is measured on its own, exactly as the judge reads it, the way probes are. Active windows (peak ≥ the activation threshold) are split into 5 bins by peak strength. Each bin gives 5 windows to detection and 5 to fuzzing, so weak activations count as much as the top ones. Detection also gets as many inactive windows.
  - *Detection.* A judge given only the explanation lists the windows it says activate the pole. Score: balanced accuracy, with recall per activation bin.
  - *Fuzzing.* In each window some tokens are marked: the active tokens, the same number of inactive tokens, or random tokens in an inactive window. The judge lists the windows whose marks are where the explanation says the pole fires. Score: balanced accuracy.
  - *Embedding.* The AUROC of the cosine between the explanation and each detection window, from a local sentence encoder (`sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2` by default). It needs no LLM.

  When no held-out window is active, detection gets inactive windows only and scores false positives, and fuzzing is skipped. The task a judge answers holds no activations. Labels are measured when the answers are scored. `openinterp reproduce` re-measures them and re-derives the scores from the stored answers (warnings only). 0.5 is chance for all three. A narrow explanation that passes review can still score low here, if most of the pole's held-out activity falls outside it.

## 5. Reproducibility requirements

A record must let a third party re-derive its verdict:

- `identifier`: model name, layer, and neuron and pole, or head.
- `head_profile.mean_output` for a head: the zero point of its strength.
- `dictionary_profile` for a dictionary feature: the weights' repository, commit and file, and the input site and activation they are read with.
- `provenance`: model revision (commit hash), dtype, corpus and its SHA-256, and the corpus sample (seed, token budget, SHA-256). It also holds snippet and token counts, selection method and seed, and harness version.
- The full probe set with labels and rationales, stored as `adversarial_test_results`.
- `verdict.policy`: the exact thresholds, including `protocol_version`.
- `verdict.environment` (and `provenance.environment` for the scan): the device, weights dtype, model commit and the openinterp, torch and transformers versions the measurements ran with.

`provenance.costs` also records what the experiment cost to run. It records wall time per stage (model load, scan, lenses, proposer, held-out windows, judge, reviewer, probe measurement, gates, causal metrics). It also records each LLM call's model, prompt size, tokens and cost as LiteLLM reports them. A prompt answered from a file (`external/<name>`) is timed from the prompt being written to the answer being written. Its tokens are recorded when a usage file sits next to the answer (`<answer>.usage.json`). `openinterp usage` writes that file from a Claude Code subagent's transcript. The tokens are priced at list price for the recorded model from a dated table (`openinterp/core/pricing.py`, recorded as `price_table`). Without a usage file, only the prompt's length is recorded. A re-review or re-evaluation records only its own costs. These numbers are reported, never gated, and `reproduce` ignores them.

`openinterp reevaluate --record <file>` re-measures a record's stored probes under the current policy, without LLM calls. The result is a new experiment.

`openinterp reproduce <file>…` (or `--base-ref origin/main` for every record a branch adds or changes) checks that a record is what it claims to be. It loads the model commit and dtype the record pins and re-runs everything the verdict rests on, without LLM calls. A record in a 16-bit dtype (bfloat16 or float16) is the exception: it re-runs in float32 (below).

- each top example's per-token strengths, and the scale (`max_activation`) that every threshold is a fraction of.
- each counterfactual's observed Δ.
- the review, with the record's own probes, reviewer and policy.
- with `--corpus <the scan's corpus>`, the corpus sample: re-drawn from its seed and budget, it must hash the same and contain every top example. A different corpus is a warning. CI has no corpus, so it skips this check.

Numbers must agree within `atol × scale + rtol × |recorded|` (defaults 0.001 and 0.01). These absorb float differences between devices and library versions, but not an edited number. Every pass/fail, every gate and the status must agree exactly. Causal metrics and joint ablation are not gated, so a difference there is only a warning. A different device or library version is reported as a warning. A record judged under another protocol version can't be reproduced by the current harness, because its decision rule was different: re-evaluate it instead.

A record in a 16-bit dtype is re-run in float32. 16-bit rounding moves a number by a few percent from one device to another, so these records are compared with wider rules (`openinterp/half_precision.py`, `docs/analyses/half-precision-reproduce.md`):

- Numbers must agree within `0.05 × scale + 0.02 × |recorded|`. At a peak that is 7%, so a 10% edit of a peak still fails.
- Up to 4 tokens of each top example may differ by more, as a warning. Deep layers on unusual text can move a few tokens by up to 22% of the peak.
- A pass/fail may flip when rounding moves its number across the threshold. That is a warning only if the numbers agree, and the recorded pass/fail follows from the recorded number. The recorded gates, balanced accuracy and status must then follow exactly from the recorded results.
- Every recorded pass/fail must follow from its own recorded number, whether or not it flips.

A trusted maintainer can instead **sign** a record (`openinterp sign <file>`, with an SSH key listed in `.github/allowed_signers`). This writes `<id>.json.sig` next to the record, over its exact bytes. `reproduce` then skips the record, reporting it as signed. An invalid signature fails like a mismatch. With `--base-ref`, the signers list is read from the base ref, so a branch can't trust its own key. A signature doesn't change the verdict, which still comes from the review protocol. It only records that the signer vouches for the record.

Each key in `.github/allowed_signers` has a validity window: `valid-after`, and `valid-before` when the key is revoked or expires. A signature is checked at the time it landed on `main`. That time is the committer date of the newest first-parent commit on `main` that added or changed the record or its `.sig` file. GitHub sets that date when it merges a pull request. A record that is not on `main` yet is checked at the current time. A listed key can make a signature that landed outside the key's window. Such a signature vouches for nothing, so `reproduce` re-runs the record and reports why. `openinterp signatures` lists the records whose signatures are not trusted in this way. `SECURITY.md` says how to rotate and revoke a key.

A nightly workflow (`.github/workflows/reproduce-nightly.yml`) runs `openinterp reproduce --sample 20` on `main`, because a library upgrade can shift activations in records that no pull request touches. The date chooses the records in rotation, so the registry is covered in about 100 days. Signed records are re-run too, and a bad signature still fails. A record from another protocol version gets its scan evidence and counterfactuals compared, without its review. The run starts no record after 140 minutes (`--time-budget`), so a night that draws several slow records still ends inside its time limit. A record not reached is listed, and waits for its next turn in the rotation. A failed run opens an issue, or comments on the open one. It changes no record or verdict.

Reproduction can't show that the reviewer wrote its probes without seeing activations. It shows only that the record's numbers and verdict follow from its probes and the model.

## 6. Changing the protocol

Any change to the decision rule or its defaults bumps `PROTOCOL_VERSION` in `core/schemas.py` and adds an entry below. Records keep the version they were judged under. Re-evaluating them is an explicit step, never a silent one.

| Version | Change |
| --- | --- |
| 0.1 | Weighted mean of probe, counterfactual and lookahead pass rates, and an absolute Δ tolerance of 0.35. |
| 0.2 | Gated rule with balanced accuracy, relative Δ tolerance, minimum probe counts, an independent-review gate and a provenance block. |
| 0.3 | Near-duplicate probes cluster into single observations. A confound-control gate compares with token, position and never-active baselines. Correlated neurons and joint ablation are recorded. |
| 0.4 | `independent_review` is recorded but no longer gated by default (`require_independent_review = False`). |
| 0.5 | Causal metrics (still not gated) are scored on claimed tokens or next-token KL at the pole's most-changed position. They are percentiles against activation-change-matched same-layer neurons, and control pairs are skipped. Replaces the fraction of the top-1-logit gap restored. |
| 0.6 | Attention heads can be audited. A head's strength is \|W_O (z − z̄)\| against its mean output over the scan, stored in the record. Its ablation is mean ablation, and its causal metrics rank it against the other heads of its layer. The decision rule for neuron poles is unchanged. `CausalMetrics.null_neurons` now records how many comparison units were used. |
| 0.7 | A scan reads a seeded sample of whole documents within a token budget (default 200,000 tokens, seed 0), recorded in `provenance.corpus_sample`, instead of the whole corpus. The scale, and so the "active" threshold, is the maximum over that sample. The decision rule is unchanged. A record without a sample scanned its whole corpus, and re-evaluating it keeps that scale. |
| 0.8 | A unit active at the first position on at least half the vocabulary has a first-token floor. It is not measured at a text's first token: its peaks, scan statistics, confound baselines, held-out windows, wiring and input attribution skip it. For it, a one-token probe or counterfactual is recorded as unmeasured and counts in no metric. A unit active on at least half its scan's tokens is default-on: the scan stops on it, and a review fails the new `not_default_on` gate (`ReviewPolicy.max_active_share`). A hypothesis that ties the confound baseline passes, as intended. A rounding mismatch had failed some ties. This settles how GPT-2's negative poles and its early-layer positive poles with a first-token floor are audited (`docs/analyses/position-zero.md`). Re-evaluating an older record keeps its scale, and judges its floor against it. |

### Registry-level dependence

These do not change the decision rule.

- **Re-measurements.** An experiment that re-measures an earlier experiment's probe set (e.g. `reevaluate`) is marked as such. It is ranked after the original and is never counted as independent evidence.
- **Most-conclusive list.** The home page shows one row per neuron pole.
- **Standing.** A unit's status, and with it every verified count and the false discovery estimate, follows the rule under §3 step 9. It reads verdicts and never changes one, so introducing it did not bump the version. A claim backed by several agreeing reviews enters the false discovery estimate once, with its weakest review's P(useful).
- **False discovery estimate.** The home page reports the expected number of false discoveries among current VERIFIED claims, Σ (1 − P(useful)) over one claim per neuron pole.
- **Dictionary features.** Adding them changed the decision rule for no unit, so it did not bump the version. Their verdicts are left out of the home page's native totals, its verified list and the false discovery estimate, and are reported separately.

## 7. Worked example

Qwen2.5-0.5B, layer 11, neuron 4255, positive pole, selected at random (`wiki/experiments/Qwen--Qwen2.5-0.5B/L11/N4255-positive/`).

- **Hypothesis:** fires on the connective that introduces examples of a category just named ("industries, *including* …", "such *as*").
- **v0.1 verdict: VERIFIED (0.91).** Ten easy negatives outweighed three failed holdouts, and Δ predictions of 0.40 and 0.30 "passed" with observed values of 0.11 and 0.08.
- **v0.2 verdict: NEEDS_REVISION.** Specificity was 1.0 but sensitivity only 0.4: "for example", "like" and "among them" did not fire. Counterfactual reproduction was 1/4. Proposer and reviewer were the same model.
- **v0.3 re-evaluation: NEEDS_REVISION.** It also fails `beats_confound_baseline`, because the scan predates confound profiling. A rescan found:
  - token identity explains 24% of activation variance and position about 0%.
  - the mean strength on " including" is 0.37, below the 0.43 "active" threshold, so the token alone does not make the neuron fire and context matters.
  - no layer-11 neuron correlates above |r| = 0.10, yet co-activation tells a different story. Ten neuron poles co-fire on 40–52 of its ~180 active tokens, a lift of at least 10–13 at the 95% lower bound. Global correlation hid them because the coupling exists only when the neuron fires.
  - Ablating the top three partners moves the output about 14× more than ablating N4255 alone. With the partners removed, N4255 adds almost nothing (redundancy ratio 0.02). Its effect overlaps a shared pathway rather than being masked.
  - Its logit-lens tokens ("oor", " Fälle", ".ordinal") were noise. The Jacobian lens shares none of them and is just as flat (kurtosis 3.0). This supports the retrospective claim rather than revealing a hidden prediction role.
- **Next submission should:** narrow the claim to the token *including* (and *e.g.*) after a category noun, and get an independent reviewer.
