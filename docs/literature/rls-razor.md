## Summary

**Claim.** RL fine-tuning and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can reach the same score on a new task, but RL keeps more of the model's earlier skills. One number predicts the forgetting: the KL divergence between the base and the fine-tuned model, measured on inputs from the new task. On-policy RL finds solutions that are close to the base model in this KL. The authors call this "RL's Razor".

**Method.**

- **Language models.** Qwen 2.5 3B-Instruct, fine-tuned on three tasks. Math is questions from Open-Reasoner-Zero. Science Q&A is the Chemistry L-3 subset of SciKnowEval. Tool use is ToolAlpaca.
- **SFT targets.** For math, the authors sampled up to 16 DeepSeek R1 answers per prompt and kept one with the right final answer. That covered 96% of the prompts. For science, they used GPT-4o, which covered all prompts.
- **RL.** GRPO with a binary success reward and no KL term. The loss type is Dr. GRPO, the group size is 64, and there are 8 prompts per generation. Training is in bfloat16.
- **Robotics.** OpenVLA 7B in SimplerEnv learns to pick up a can. Forgetting is the score on the open and close drawer tasks. SFT copies successful RT-1 trajectories. RL is REINFORCE with a reward normalization baseline.
- **Forgetting.** For the language models, the mean of HellaSwag, TruthfulQA, MMLU, IFEval, Winogrande and HumanEval, from the Language Model Evaluation Harness.
- **Sweeps and frontiers.** "Dozens of models" for each method over learning rate, scheduler, epochs and batch size (Table 2). The authors keep the models within 2 accuracy points of the Pareto frontier, and fit an exponential curve to them.
- **ParityMNIST.** A toy setting, so that runs can go to convergence. A 3-layer MLP (785, 512, 256, 10) is first trained on 500 ParityMNIST and 500 FashionMNIST images. An input flag tells the two tasks apart. Fine-tuning is on ParityMNIST, where any digit label of the right parity counts as right. Forgetting is the FashionMNIST score.
- **ParityMNIST methods.** GRPO, GRPO with a KL term of 0.1, two SFT labelings and an "oracle" SFT. SFT 1 maps even digits to 0 and odd digits to 1. SFT 2 maps even digits to 0 or 4, and odd digits to 1 or 5. The oracle samples labels from the distribution nearest in KL to the base model among those with full accuracy. Each method sweeps 15 learning rates, two schedulers and 1 or 2 epochs. With mid-training checkpoints, that gives about 500 runs per method.
- **Other predictors.** Weight change (L1, Fisher-weighted L2 and spectral norm), and activation change (L1 and L2). Sparsity and rank of the update, and four output distances on new-task inputs. The output distances are forward KL, reverse KL, total variation and the L2 distance. The Fisher uses inputs from the old task. Each predictor gets a second-degree polynomial fit to forgetting, scored by R².
- **On-policy or offline.** On Science Q&A, four objectives fill a two-by-two grid: on-policy or offline, and with or without negative examples. They are GRPO, 1–0 Reinforce (SFT on the model's own right answers), SFT and SimPO.
- **Theory.** Take a finite output set and a binary reward. Then rejection sampling gives the distribution nearest to the policy in KL among those with full reward (Lemma 5.1). Policy gradient is then an EM procedure with information projections (Theorem A.3 in v1, B.3 in the camera-ready). For an exponential family, it converges to the optimal policy nearest in KL to the start (Theorem 5.2).
- **More analyses.** CKNNA similarity of representations on Wikipedia paragraphs, and SFT on Qwen 2.5 Instruct at 3B, 7B and 14B. Also the alignment of single update steps with the old task's gradient.

**Findings, as the source reports them.** All numbers are from the text or from Table 1 of the camera-ready.

## Where the claims go beyond the evidence

- **The numbers for the law come mostly from a toy.** The R² of 0.96 and the whole ranking of predictors in Table 1 come from one 3-layer MLP on ParityMNIST. The pretraining set was 1,000 images. On the language models, the fit drops to R² = 0.71, from one figure, with no count of points. The authors call the language-model fit "weaker", but the abstract says the KL "determined" the forgetting.
- **The KL direction is not one thing.** The law uses E KL(π₀‖π), which section 6 calls forward KL. Theorem 5.2 picks the policy that minimises KL(π‖π₀), the reverse direction. The appendix defines the oracle as the minimiser of KL(π₀‖q) under full accuracy. But as written, that KL is infinite for every q with no mass on wrong labels, whenever π₀ gives them any mass. The camera-ready says that the oracle is the rejection-sampling distribution, which minimises the other direction. So the theory explains a small reverse KL, and the law measures forward KL. The camera-ready's change to "both forward and reverse" narrows this gap, but does not close it.
- **The language-model KL is an estimate.** The text names no estimator, no sample size and no token set for the KL of a 3B model on generated text. The authors put the weaker fit down to "noise from approximate KL and accuracy estimation".
- **No seeds and few intervals.** Table 1 gives ± values, but the text does not say what they are over. The language-model frontiers use one exponential fit to points within 2 accuracy points of the frontier. The authors chose the filter and the curve form. The text gives no intervals for the frontiers and no count of runs, only "dozens".
- **SFT here is distillation from a distant model.** The math and science SFT targets come from DeepSeek R1 and GPT-4o. So "SFT" also means "imitate a different model's style". The oracle and the RL-teacher tests that separate the target from the method are on ParityMNIST only.
- **The RL settings disagree within the camera-ready.** Section 3.1 says the RL runs take "single gradient steps". Table 2 still sweeps the number of GRPO iterations over 1 and 2.
- **Two captions disagree across versions.** For the left panel of the single-step plot, v1 says "anti-correlated" and the camera-ready says "uncorrelated". The camera-ready also refers to "Figure D.1" for a figure that has the number 8. The v1 text has the same kind of slip with "Figure C.1".
- **The scale test covers SFT only.** Appendix D.2 runs SFT at 3B, 7B and 14B, but not RL. So it does not show that the RL–SFT gap holds with scale.
- **Forgetting is a benchmark mean.** The mean of six benchmarks can hide a large loss on one. The authors say that benchmark scores can confuse a lost skill with a format mismatch, and they add the CKNNA analysis for that reason. That analysis gives one number per method, from one pair of models, and the text does not name the layer.
- **The theory is for a simple case.** It assumes a finite output set, a binary reward and a convex or exponential-family policy class. The authors state that a neural network's policy class is not in general of this kind, so exact convergence may fail. The camera-ready adds that a full account of deep networks is future work.

The authors state other limits too. They have no mechanism for why a large KL on the new task harms old skills. They have not tested frontier scales. They did not study off-policy online algorithms.

## Use in this repo

- **The repo has no fine-tuning code.** [`fixing-models.md`](fixing-models.md) notes that the repo has no fine-tuning or weight-edit code. So the paper's RL and SFT runs cannot be repeated here as they are. The repo can still read the paper's predictors on model pairs that already exist.
- **The paper scores a model, not a unit.** Its forgetting is a benchmark mean, and its KL is one number for each fine-tuned model. It says nothing about neurons, explanations or probes. The repo's question in #5118 is different: given one fine-tune, which VERIFIED poles keep? The paper's law gives one point per model pair, so it cannot rank the poles of one pair.
- **Two different KLs.** Each record already stores `lookahead.kl_divergence` (`openinterpretability/harness/lookahead.py`). That is KL(clean‖ablated), the effect of ablating one pole on the next-token distribution. The paper's KL compares a base model with its fine-tuned model. The two must not be confused. The first is a measure of a pole's importance, and a comment on #5118 already proposes it as a predictor.
- **The paper's predictor ranking bears on #5118 and #5133.** On ParityMNIST, Fisher-weighted weight change gives R² 0.58 and L1 weight change 0.34, against 0.96 for KL. #5118 tests the diagonal Fisher as a predictor, and #5133 tests the size of the weight change at a pole. The paper predicts the forgetting of a whole model, so its result does not show that these predictors fail for single poles. It does give a reason to run an output-side predictor beside them, against the same null.
- **The repo's own activation measure already fails to separate.** The paper finds activation change a weak predictor (R² 0.52 to 0.55). In [`docs/analyses/base-instruct.md`](../analyses/base-instruct.md), on Qwen2.5-1.5B, the base–instruct correlation does not separate the poles that keep (median 0.983) from those that fail (median 0.986). That analysis is about poles, not models, so this is a similar result, not a test of the paper.
- **The paper's "new task" for instruction tuning is chat text.** The law measures KL on the new task's own inputs. For a base–instruct pair, those are chat-formatted prompts. The base-instruct analysis finds that the chat template costs 9 to 18 points of keep share. It is 88% to 70% on 1.5B, and 81% to 72% on 0.5B. #5126 asks whether poles move more on chat-formatted text. The paper suggests a measure for that question: the base–instruct output KL on chat text, next to the plain-text KL.
- **The repo's poles move little, as in the paper's RL runs.** On Qwen2.5-0.5B, the median base–instruct activation correlation is 0.974, and on Qwen2.5-1.5B it is 0.983. The paper's CKNNA is 0.94 for RL and 0.56 for SFT. The measures differ: one is a single neuron's activations, the other is the geometry of a layer. The analysis does not record the training recipe of the Qwen2.5 instruct models. So this page does not say which side of the paper they are on.
- **#5125 could test the law with registry units.** #5125 asks whether VERIFIED explanations keep through a sequence of narrow fine-tunes. If it runs, each fine-tune gives one point: the KL on that fine-tune's data, and the share of poles that keep. That is the paper's law with the keep share in place of a benchmark score. It would also give several points per base model, where base-instruct gives one.
- **Pretraining shows the same gap between weights and behaviour.** In [`papers/neuron-development/`](../../papers/neuron-development/README.md), Pythia-70m's loss is within 1% of its final value by step 16,000. Its 86 VERIFIED poles still correlate with their final form at a median of only 0.62 there. The paper finds that large weight changes can come without forgetting. In both, the size of the weight change and the change in behaviour come apart.
- **Evidence for the fix campaign, not a design.** A fix for the #4781 " days" error would be a narrow fine-tune. The paper suggests two things that bear on it. Imitation of an external target forgets more than on-policy updates, and an explicit KL term helps on-policy updates but barely helps SFT. The KL on the fix's own data can be read during training without old-task data. [`fixing-models.md`](fixing-models.md) holds the repo's synthesis on fixes.
- **A question for the maintainer.** Should a weight fix report its KL to the base model on the fix's own data, next to the keep share of VERIFIED poles? That is a choice in the fix campaign, so this page records it as a question and opens no issue.
- **A follow-up that trains nothing.** For each Qwen2.5 base–instruct pair, read the next-token KL between the two models. Read it on each pole's own probes and on its top contexts in `corpus.jsonl`. Read it on plain text and inside the chat template. Then test whether a pole's KL predicts whether it keeps, in the existing rows, against a null of shuffled poles. It can share its rows and its null with #5118 and #5133. Then one test compares the output-side, Fisher and weight-change predictors on the same poles.
