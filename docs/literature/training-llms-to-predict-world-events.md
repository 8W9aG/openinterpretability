# Training LLMs to Predict World Events

**Citation.** Scott Jeen, Matthew Aitchison and Mantic. "Training LLMs to Predict World Events". Thinking Machines Lab: News, 19 March 2026. [Post](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/training-llms-to-predict-world-events/), [test questions](https://gist.github.com/enjeeneer/86e24a52e6041a3d78e333bcab16984d).

**Status.** Not applicable. It is a guest post by a forecasting company, on the lab's training product (Tinker). It reports no peer review, and links no code, weights or training data.

**Read.** The post's full text and the question list, on 2026-11-07. The figures are images and were not read, so numbers below come from the text and captions.

## Summary

**Claim.** Reinforcement learning on forecasting questions lifts gpt-oss-120b to the level of frontier LLMs, and the result disagrees with them enough to help an ensemble.

**Method.**

- *System.* Two phases. Deep research agents collect context into a prompt. A prediction LLM then writes chain-of-thought and sets a mixture model for when the event next occurs. The mixture's CDF at the question's date is the forecast.
- *Training data.* "~10k questions about whether an event will happen by a given date", from August 2024 to December 2025, generated by an LLM pipeline. The research phase is run once per question and stored, so training sees static prompts.
- *Training.* A "standard policy gradient algorithm with GRPO-style advantage normalisation and no division by the standard deviation". It has an importance sampling correction on the advantages, because sampling and training backends can disagree on token probabilities. Group size 8, batch size 64. The post doesn't state a learning rate, LoRA rank or step count.
- *Reward.* The Brier score. The authors found it "leads to more stable training than the log score". They suggest, without testing it, that this is because it is bounded and gives lower-variance gradients.
- *Test.* Unseen questions from the Metaculus Q2 2025 AI Benchmark, scored by the Metaculus baseline score. This score is log score rescaled so that 100 is perfect and 0 is a 50% forecast. They passed over the Fall 2025 tournament for its "lower quality questions", and compare only models with a knowledge cutoff before the tournament.

**Findings, as the post reports them.**

| Result | Number |
| --- | --- |
| gpt-oss-120b, before and after fine-tuning | 38.6 → 45.8 mean baseline points per question |
| Constant forecast of 18.8% (the Q1 2025 yes rate) | 22.3 |
| Gain from training, with research and mixture tools | "7 points" |
| Gain from training, without them | "only 3 points" |
| Best 5-sample ensemble | fine-tuned gpt-oss-120b 40%, Gemini 3 Pro 20%, GPT-5 20%, Grok 4 20% |
| Best 3-way ensembles | fine-tuned, Gemini 3 Pro, GPT-5: 56%, 26%, 18%. Fine-tuned, Gemini 3 Pro, Grok 4: 48%, 26%, 26% |

Before training the model is "below all frontier models", and after it "marginally above". Removing a model from the best ensemble and re-optimising costs most for Grok 4 and second most for the fine-tuned model. Without research and tools, the model "never reaches the initial score" of the model with them.

The post reports no activation, probe, feature or neuron analysis. Its "decorrelated" claim rests on Jensen-Shannon divergence between forecasts and on ensemble weights.

## Where the claims go beyond the evidence

These are our observations, not the post's. The post may have handled some of them in ways it doesn't describe.

- **The training window contains the test window.** The published questions have forecast times from 2025-04-21 to 2025-06-15 and resolve by 2025-07-03. Training questions run from August 2024 to December 2025. The post says the test questions are unseen, but not that training questions resolving in or after that period were removed. If they were kept, the weights can absorb outcomes from the test period, even with no shared question. The knowledge-cutoff rule applies to the models' starting points, not to this training.
- **The research phase isn't shown to be date-limited.** The post doesn't say that search results for a question were restricted to before its forecast time. Any leak here would raise scores for every model given that research.
- **The test set is small, and no intervals are reported.** The list has 113 questions, 25 of which resolved yes. A constant forecast's per-question score there has a standard deviation of about 88, so a standard error of about 8 points (our calculation). Paired differences between models would be tighter, but the post gives none, so "marginally above" is not an established ranking.
- **Selection on the test set.** Ensemble weights are called "optimal" and are reported on the benchmark questions, with no held-out set mentioned. The post shows the test score during training and says that "training continued for further steps but performance no longer improved". It does not say how the final checkpoint was chosen. If the test set chose either one, the score is optimistic.
- **The constant-forecast figure doesn't quite reproduce.** Scoring 18.8% on the published list with the post's definition, `100 × (1 + log2 p)`, gives 23.3, not 22.3. A difference in question sets could explain this. It is small, but so is the margin over frontier models.
- **No repeated runs are reported.** The post shows one curve per setup and gives no seeds. It reads the 7-against-3 comparison as "these prerequisites positively influence the optimization dynamics", but without repeats that can't be separated from run-to-run variation.

## Use in this repo

**Not applicable as a method.** The repo explains native units of pretrained models, and its verdicts come from probes run on a pinned model revision (`docs/PROTOCOL.md`). The post doesn't look inside any model, and forecasting accuracy isn't something the registry measures.

**The fine-tuned model can't be studied.** A base-versus-fine-tuned comparison would fit the model diffing item in `TODO.md` (*Investigate model concept merging or diffing*), but no weights are linked. The planned ground-truth item (`TODO.md`, *Ground-truth calibration*) needs a small model fine-tuned to plant a known neuron. This post's change to the model is unknown, so it can't serve there either. The base model is already a `TODO.md` candidate for other reasons (*GPT-OSS*), and this post doesn't change its priority.

**Two evaluation habits carry over.** Neither needs new code now.

- *Split by time when predicting from the registry.* The planned model that predicts whether a neuron will verify (`TODO.md`, *Predict whether an unstudied neuron will verify*) trains on registry verdicts with a held-out split. The overlap above is the failure to avoid. Protocol versions, reviewer agents and corpora all change over the registry's life, and many records share a unit. A split should hold out later records and whole units, not random rows.
- *Score that predictor with a proper rule, against a base rate.* The post's most useful reference line is the constant forecast at last period's rate, 22.3 points rather than 0. The same item says "if it beats chance". For a skewed outcome like VERIFIED, chance should mean the base rate from earlier records. A Brier or log score would reward calibration as well as ranking. The post's preference for Brier in training is an untested guess, and doesn't bear on which to report.

**Leave-one-out as a question for reviewers.** Removing a model and re-optimising measures what it adds beyond the others. [Reviewer-effect crossover](../analyses/reviewer-crossover.md) already shows that the reviewer accounts for a small but real share of verdicts (6–8% of the variance). Asking which reviewer's probes catch failures that the others miss would need a set of known false hypotheses. [Null calibration](../analyses/null-calibration.md) has one in its swapped units. `TODO.md` (*Protocol and science*, *Measure which reviewers catch false hypotheses*) now plans it.

**What doesn't carry over.** Policy-gradient training, the Brier reward and the importance sampling correction are all training details. The repo doesn't train models for its verdicts. The SPD runs in `papers/` train decompositions, which have no reinforcement learning step.
