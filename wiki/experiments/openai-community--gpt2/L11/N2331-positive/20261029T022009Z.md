The kitchen cupboards held the usual archaeology: a tin of cloves, three rolling pins, a jar of buttons, and a set of cups that matched nothing. We kept the buttons and the cups and threw out the rest, and she wrote a note to herself about the plumber, which she then lost.

It was in the cellar that the tone changed. The previous owner had kept a workshop down there, and among the rusted tools we found a rack of glass reagent bottles, a brass microscope with a cracked objective, and a box of slides labelled in a hand none of us recognised. My aunt remembered that the man had worked for a water company and that he had tested samples from the river every week. We carried the microscope upstairs and set it on the kitchen table, where it stood for a month before anyone thought to look through it.

By the autumn the house was empty and the keys had been handed over. She kept the slides and the microscope, and gave away almost everything else. I still think about the labels on those slides, written by a stranger in a cellar fifty years ago, and about how much of a life survives in the ordinary things someone leaves behind.” (activation 0.827)
