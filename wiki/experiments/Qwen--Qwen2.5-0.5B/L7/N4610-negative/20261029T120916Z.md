So the problem was in the redirect, not in storage. The front end had been refactored during the sprint to build links from a shared helper, and the helper was passing the filename through a function that escaped it a second time. The first escape turned the space into an encoded sequence and the slash into another; the second escape then encoded the percent sign itself, so the gateway received a path that matched nothing on disk. In staging we had tested with filenames that contained no spaces, which is why the defect survived review.

I wrote a short reproduction, attached it to the ticket, and left a note asking the author of the helper to make the escaping idempotent. While I waited, I walked the support team through a workaround: rename the export to something without a space, or copy the download from the admin console instead of the dashboard. Both were ugly, and neither would scale to the several hundred reports that finance generated at the end of every quarter.

The fix landed the next afternoon. It was four lines long. The helper now checks whether the value is already encoded before escaping it, and the test suite includes a filename with a space and a slash so that the same mistake cannot return quietly. I closed the ticket, wrote a paragraph in the postmortem, and added the incident to the list of things that only appear when real names meet real web addresses.

Looking back, the frustrating part is not the bug itself but the three hours we spent arguing about caches. The evidence was in the logs the whole time, written in the language of status codes and headers, and it took a second pair of eyes to read it. The dashboard is fine now. The lesson is the one we keep relearning: test the names that users actually give their files, not the tidy names invented for a staging environment.” (activation 0.389)
