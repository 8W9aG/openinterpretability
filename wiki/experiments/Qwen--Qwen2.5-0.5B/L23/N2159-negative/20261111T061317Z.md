The owner at the time had signed a consent order and paid a penalty, and then the file had gone quiet. Nobody at the town office remembered any of it. The wells had been tested every year since, and every year the results had come back clean, but the tests had not looked for the right compounds.

By November the council had hired a second firm, and the engineers had produced a report that ran to eighty pages. The report recommended a phased program: first a monitoring plan with quarterly sampling, then a treatment system using granular activated carbon, and finally a connection to the regional water authority if the concentrations did not fall. The capital cost was estimated at 4.2 million dollars, which was more than the town's entire annual budget. Someone asked whether the state would help. The answer was that Millbrook might qualify for a low-interest loan from the revolving fund, but only if the town adopted an ordinance restricting new wells in the affected area and only if the system remained in non-compliance with the secondary standard for another year.

The rate study arrived in January. It showed that the median household income in Millbrook was well below the county average, and that a full cost-of-service increase would push the average quarterly bill past what many families could pay. The consultants proposed a tiered schedule, with a lower rate for the first ten thousand gallons and a higher rate for anything above that. Doris read the whole thing aloud, twice, because two residents had asked her to. When the vote came, it was unanimous, and nobody in the room looked happy about it. Outside, the snow had started again, and the parking lot was already white.

At the next meeting the only item on the agenda was a letter from the Department of Public Works confirming that the new signs had been ordered. Doris read it, wrote it in the minutes, and closed her notebook at eight minutes past nine. Someone brought cookies. The meeting was, for once, short.” (activation 2.010)
