For most of the year the villages along it are quiet places, where fishing boats are pulled up on the sand before dawn and the markets close by noon. The monsoon changes everything. From June until September the rain arrives in sheets, the rivers swell and turn the colour of coffee, and the narrow roads that link one town to the next become ribbons of mud that swallow carts and buses alike. Visitors who come in the dry season often remark on the heat, the palm trees, the brightly painted houses with their red tile roofs, and the unhurried pace of life, but they rarely see the coast as the people who live there see it, waiting for the first clouds to gather over the water.

Historians have argued for a century about the importance of these ports in the trade between the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf and the Malabar coast. Pepper, cardamom, teak and coir moved through the harbours for more than two thousand years, and the customs records of the smaller ports, where they survive, describe a traffic in goods that is hard to reconcile with the modest size of the settlements that produced them. Some of the ledgers were kept by clerks who wrote in three languages and who recorded the weight of every sack, the name of every vessel and the fee paid to every broker, and their careful columns are among the few sources that allow historians to compare prices across the region.

The annual festival in the temple town draws families from the surrounding villages. For three days the streets fill with music, the smell of frying bread and the cries of traders selling cloth, brass lamps and sweets. Young men carry the image of the deity through the lanes on a decorated cart, stopping at each house to receive offerings of rice and flowers, while the older women sit on the steps and argue about which family should lead the procession next year. When the drums fall silent in the evening, the crowd drifts home along the beach, and for an hour the only sounds are the surf and the laughter of children running ahead with lanterns.

It was during a survey of the backwaters in 1894 that a colonial engineer, working from a small boat with a lead line and a compass, recorded a depth of only four fathoms at the bar off Kadinamkulam, a measurement that later forced the government to move the customs house several miles to the south. His notebooks, preserved in the district archive, contain sketches of the channel, lists of the tides and a long complaint about the mosquitoes.

Today the coast is changing faster than at any time since the canal was dug. New bridges cross the backwaters, lorries run through the night, and the old warehouses near the water have been converted into guest houses for travellers who arrive with maps and leave with photographs.
