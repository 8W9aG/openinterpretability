Every election season the same scene repeats. A campaign releases an internal survey showing its candidate ahead by three points, a rival's survey shows the opposite, and commentators spend the week arguing about which set of numbers to believe. The dispute is older than the telephone, and it is not really about politics. It is about how a sample of a thousand people can say anything trustworthy about a nation of millions.

The short answer is that randomness, handled carefully, cancels itself out. A well-drawn sample is not a miniature copy of the country; it is a random selection from it, and random selections wobble. Wobble is measurable. If you could repeat the same survey a hundred times with fresh respondents each time, the results would scatter around the truth in a pattern that statisticians can describe precisely, and the width of that scatter is what pollsters report as the margin of error.

That description raises an obvious objection. Nobody can afford to run the same survey a hundred times, and even if they could, the country would have changed in the meantime. So how does a pollster know the shape of the scatter without repeating the exercise? The answer is a result proved in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and sharpened by later mathematicians: the central limit theorem says roughly that when you add up many small independent influences, the total tends toward a particular bell-shaped distribution, whatever the influences looked like individually. That single fact turns an unknowable pile of individual quirks into a predictable envelope, and it is why a survey can attach a number to its own uncertainty.

The practical caveats matter as much as the theory. A margin of error covers sampling error only. It says nothing about households that never answer the phone, about voters who change their minds in the final week, or about the temptation for a campaign to publish only the survey it likes. When several pollsters report results that are suspiciously close together, the pattern may reflect herding rather than consensus, because nobody wants to be the outlier on election morning. Careful shops therefore publish their full questionnaires, their weighting decisions and the dates of their fieldwork, so that readers can judge the survey rather than simply the headline.

History offers a useful warning. In 1936 a magazine mailed millions of ballots and confidently predicted the wrong winner, because the people who returned the ballots were not a random sample of the electorate. A smaller poll that trusted a properly drawn sample got the answer right. Size matters less than method, and confidence matters less than candour.

None of this makes polling a science of prophecy. It makes it a discipline of honest uncertainty, in which a number arrives with an error bar attached and a method attached to the error bar. The next time a candidate's lead is described as three points plus or minus four, the reader who understands where that envelope came from is harder to fool.” (activation 0.503)
