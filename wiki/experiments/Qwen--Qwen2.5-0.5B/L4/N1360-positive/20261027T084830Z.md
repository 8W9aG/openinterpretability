## Hypothesis

> N1360+ is a retrospective 'name-completion' detector: it fires on the token at which a specific, familiar name for a concrete thing becomes complete - the whole token when the name is a single token ('calcium', 'rabbits', 'Sudan', 'Austria', 'diabetes') and the word-final subword piece when the name is spelled across tokens ('occus' in Streptococcus, 'ella' in Salmonella, 'oodle' in Poodle, 'cloth' in dishcloth) - including the digit that completes a written year ('0' in 'the late 1800s'). It is strongest (0.8-1.4 on the pole's 2.11 scale) when two to four such names of things in the world - substances and foods, organisms, diseases and pathogens, products - are enumerated in one expository sentence, and it falls below 0.5 when those names are replaced by generic category words or abstractions ('light, exercise or sleep', 'a first organism, a second organism') or when the word is one the model has no whole-word entry for (pangolin, okapi, bassoon, and quartz/feldspar in a plain list of minerals).

Revision of [20261026T005048Z](20261026T005048Z.md), narrowed from "specific
reference" to "the token where a familiar concrete name completes". It did **worse** on the
failed gate, not better: sensitivity 0.29 (2/7) against 0.43 before, balanced accuracy 0.57
against 0.64, with the never-active baseline still ahead. Specificity held at 0.85 and all
eight counterfactuals reproduced, so the claim is measurable; it is just not the feature.

The two holdouts that fired are the ones the claim predicts from *word shape* rather than
reference: `tablecloth` (0.836), where the peak is the `cloth` subword piece, and the
long booklet passage (0.748), which ends on `honey, cider, and maple syrup`. The failures
are all plausible "familiar concrete name" lists — `lentils, barley, and cumin` (0.417),
`measles, rubella, and mumps` (0.381), `magnesium, potassium, and calcium` (0.327),
`Klebsiella and Enterobacter` (0.340), `late 1880s` (0.499). A list of familiar concrete
names is exactly the situation the hypothesis says fires, and it does not.

The most telling datum is a token-identity contradiction: `calcium` peaks at 1.55 in the
scan passage ("deficiency of vitamin D, calcium or phosphorus") but the Ward-protocol probe
that contains the same token tops out at 0.327, so activation is not carried by the token
alone. Both firing holdouts also sit at the high end of the length scale (the booklet
passage is ~500 tokens), and the scan examples that motivated the original claim are all
long windows; short sentences with the same content routinely land at 0.3–0.5. That points
at a context-gain or passage-level component that neither hypothesis modelled.

The specificity failures are informative in the same direction: `bassoon, contrabassoon,
and oboe` (0.575) and the camel-case identifiers `BillingService, AuthProxy, CacheManager`
(0.649) both fire although neither is a "name of a thing in the world" in the intended
sense. Rare/compound word shape — the kind that splits into word-final subword pieces — is
what these share with `occus`, `oodle`, `cloth`, and `es`. A future hypothesis should test
a word-shape / subword-completion account with a *passage-length* term, and should treat
`calcium` in the two contexts above as the sharpest available discriminator. Held-out
explanation scores fell to detection 0.66 (FPR 0.52), fuzzing 0.63, AUROC 0.48, matching
the probes: the written claim does not predict this pole's activity.
