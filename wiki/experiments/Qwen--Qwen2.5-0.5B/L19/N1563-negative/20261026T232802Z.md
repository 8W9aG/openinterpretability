She was not a sentimental person and she did not write down what she felt about any of it. The ledger was a record, and records, she said, are for other people.

I found it in a cupboard after she died, under a stack of tablecloths that still smelled faintly of her kitchen. For a long time I did nothing with it. I was busy, or I told myself I was busy, and there is a kind of grief that only becomes available when you stop moving, which I was not ready to do. Then one winter the power went out for four days, and I sat down with the ledger and began to copy the entries into a notebook of my own.

The first thing I noticed was how many of the boats had names that meant nothing to me. The second was how often she had written the word same in the weather column, as though the sea had been too dull to describe. She was, I think, making a joke that only she would get, and she was making it in a document she expected no one to read. That is a particular kind of humour, and it may be the best kind.

The village is different now. There is a car park where the slipway used to be, a cafe that serves coffee in cups too large to finish, and a sign explaining that the harbour was once the busiest on this stretch of coast. The sign is accurate in the way that signs are accurate: everything it says is true and none of it is the point. My grandmother would have read it and said nothing, which from her was a full review.

I finished copying the entries in the spring. It took longer than it should have, because I kept stopping to check whether a name forty pages back was the same person, and because the handwriting changed in small ways I had not expected, as though the hand itself had aged in the margins. When it was done I put the notebook in the cupboard beside the ledger and closed the door. I have not opened either since. But I think about those four days of darkness more often than I think about most things, and I have begun to suspect that the copying was not for her at all.
