Still, she climbed the stairs slowly that evening, stopping at the window where the gulls nested, and she stood for a while with her hand on the cold iron rail.

The lighthouse itself was older than the town. It had been built after a wreck in which forty-one people died within sight of the shore, and the first keeper had been a naval man who kept a journal of everything: the ships that passed, the birds that struck the glass, the arguments with the inspector, the price of oil. That journal was in the county archive now, and Mira had read it one winter, amused and a little moved by how much of it was complaint. The man had hated the fog. He had counted the days of it the way other people count money.

When the light went electric in 1953, the keepers stopped trimming and started testing. When the last resident keeper retired in 1978, the job became a rota. Mira was the final name on the rota, and when she turned the key in the door that night the lamp was already doing its work without her, turning and turning above the black water, indifferent as a star.

Someone had brought bottles. People stood in the grass and talked about storms they remembered, and a councillor made a short speech that no one could hear properly. Mira did not make a speech. She walked down to the waterline, where the light was brightest on the stones, and looked back up at the tower the way a visitor would.

At midnight the crowd thinned. The lamp turned. Somewhere below the cliff a fishing boat came round the headland and held its course, and the beam found it, and held it, and let it go.
