The conservators expect the work to finish in the autumn, after which the staircase will reopen to the public. Visitors who want to see the ceiling before then can book a place on one of the twice-weekly tours, which run on Tuesday and Thursday afternoons and last about forty minutes.

The museum's new ceramics wing, which opened quietly in March, houses a stunning collection of eighteenth-century slipware, together with a smaller group of studio pots made in the county during the last fifty years. The displays are arranged by workshop rather than by date, and the labels, written by the curator, explain the firing techniques and the local clays that give the pieces their colour. A low case near the window holds a set of wassail cups that were used at the harvest supper until the 1930s.

School groups visit on weekday mornings, and the education officer runs a session on clay and glaze that ends with each child making a small tile to take home. The tiles are fired in a kiln in the basement and can be collected a fortnight later. Teachers who want to book a session should contact the office at least three weeks in advance, as the diary fills quickly in the summer term.

The museum shop stocks postcards, local history pamphlets and a small range of replica tiles based on the slipware in the new wing. Volunteers staff the desk at weekends and can answer questions about the building, the collection and the family history of the merchants who founded it. A printed guide, revised last year, is available at the entrance for two pounds.

Parking in the town centre is limited on Saturdays, and the nearest public car park fills by eleven. The museum is a ten minute walk from the bus station, and the number seven service stops on the high street. Access to the ground floor is step free, and a lift serves the first floor galleries, though the staircase itself remains closed while the conservation work continues.

For those planning a longer visit, the archive reading room on the second floor is open by appointment on Wednesdays and Thursdays. The archivist asks that requests be sent by email a week ahead, listing the boxes you wish to consult, since many of the records are kept off site. Photography is permitted without flash, and a photocopying service is available for a small charge.

The museum closes at half past four on weekdays and at five at weekends, and it is shut on Mondays apart from bank holidays. Admission to the permanent galleries is free, though a donation of five pounds is suggested for the special exhibition, which runs until the end of September.
