## Hypothesis

> The negative pole fires on the penal outcome of a criminal case: the punishment a court imposes or carries out on a person and the guilt it rests on. Its strongest triggers are penalty phrases - 'sentenced/imposed/condemned to' a term ('life', 'death', 'twelve years', 'eleven months', prison, probation), 'life sentence', 'death sentence', 'death penalty', 'capital punishment', parole, the execution or lethal injection of a condemned prisoner, and the reduction or appeal of a penalty ('reduced/lessened/commuted/vacated a sentence') - together with the guilt finding ('pleaded guilty', 'plea of guilty', 'guilty verdict', 'verdict was guilty', a conviction being appealed, 'guilt', and graded serious charges such as 'first degree', 'capital', 'aggravated'). Because the trigger is the penal phrase, the same words with no penal left context ('The sentence was announced', 'Executions are carried out at dawn') stay near zero, and strength grows as several cues accumulate. It is the complement of the positive pole, which fires when the authorities take a person into restraint (arrest, custody, detention, jail, apprehension - all near zero here), and it stays near zero on crimes named without a penalty (murder, rape, fraud, burglary), on investigation and charging procedure (indictment, arraignment, prosecution, testimony, jury instructions), on civil litigation and money remedies (contract, damages, negligence, fines, restitution), and on the everyday senses of the same words ('sentence' of a paragraph, 'execution' of a plan or contract, 'life' in the ocean, 'death' of a patient, 'degree' in mathematics); a few court-room words from the legal documents it fires in ('public defender', 'defense attorney', 'assistance of counsel', 'vacate', 'handed down') reach only the low active range. Its output direction promotes this same penal lexicon rather than a specific next token, so the mechanism is retrospective.
