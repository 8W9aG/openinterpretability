Sediment cores lifted from abyssal plains contain tiny shells that record millions of years of ocean chemistry, allowing scientists to reconstruct temperature, circulation and surface productivity across deep time.

The engineering challenges are considerable. Pressure at the bottom of the Mariana Trench is immense, and instruments must survive corrosive seawater and near-freezing temperatures. Titanium housings, oil-filled compensators and fibre-optic tethers have gradually extended the reach of human observation. A modern remotely operated vehicle can descend for twelve hours, collect samples with manipulator arms and stream high-definition video to a control room thousands of kilometres away. Recordings from these dives are archived and shared openly, so that a student in Lisbon can analyse footage gathered above the Kermadec Trench.

Advances in genomics have transformed the field as well. DNA extracted from a single amphipod can now be sequenced within a day, revealing which genes are switched on at abyssal pressures. Comparative studies suggest that several lineages independently evolved similar solutions, from stabilising proteins to accumulating osmolytes that protect membranes from collapse. Genetic tools have also made it possible to identify larvae drifting in the water column, linking adults on one seamount to juveniles on another hundreds of kilometres away. Such findings hint at corridors of connectivity that conservation planners are only beginning to map.

Explaining how animals sense their surroundings at such depths requires patient physiological work. In the laboratory, researchers keep specimens in darkened tanks and measure the neurophysiology of pressure tolerance.” (activation 7.665)
