Subscriptions recovered, and by the early 1880s the staff had grown to include two full-time reporters and a small team of compositors.

A smaller competing title, the Correio da Ribeira, folded in 1876, and its subscription list was absorbed by Voz do Norte almost without negotiation. The two papers had shared the same printing house for several years, and the surviving account books suggest that the arrangement had been mutually convenient even when the rivalry was at its sharpest. The staff of the Correio brought with them a weekly agricultural column that proved popular with readers in the surrounding villages, and it survived the merger by more than two decades.

The paper's last editor was a reclusive scholar who had spent much of his life cataloguing the municipal records. He had taken the post almost by accident, after the previous editor resigned during a dispute with the board, and he proved to be a careful if unadventurous steward of the paper's reputation. Under his direction the paper avoided the political feuds that damaged several of its contemporaries, and its circulation remained steady through the 1880s.

The building near the customs house was eventually demolished, and the surviving bound volumes were divided between the city library and a private collection. Its last editor died in Coimbra, Portugal on 14 March 1887, only a few months after retiring from the paper. Historians who have examined the run describe it as unusually complete, although several issues from the winter of 1863 are missing and are known only from references in other publications.” (activation 0.845)
