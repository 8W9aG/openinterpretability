## Protocol and science

- [ ] Find why L2 and L3 nearly shut when only the block LayerNorms read a constant (`papers/why-neurons-rest/README.md`, *Where the push comes from*). In `relu_final_norm_centred_dropout_0`, h's SD at L2 and L3 fell to 0.07–0.10, and the loss was worse. The later blocks use a constant in the residual stream, and it lowers their loss (*What the final LayerNorm does with a constant*). Check whether, without the final norm's pull, the MLPs' mean output grows until it fills the block norms' inputs. Also split dropout's part in the pull into its routes, as `scripts/ln_offset.py` splits the final norm's.
- [ ] Find why the output's push of GELU and SiLU points up most on tokens below the rest. That makes their spread term point down, and in GELU's dip it pushes h down directly (`papers/why-neurons-rest/README.md`, *Where the push comes from*, `training_spread_bins`). The steepest tokens push up. Record the output's push itself for each bin of h, not only its part of the spread term. Then check whether the pattern stays when the final LayerNorm and dropout can't see a constant, with a GELU version of the norm conditions.
- [ ] Finish the test of Pythia's training setup (`papers/why-neurons-rest/README.md`, *Pythia's training setup, one part at a time*). At Pythia-70m's checkpoints the push points down in 13 of 30 layers and checkpoints, and its sign matches each neuron's next move at chance. Pythia's warm-up and AdamW settings don't weaken the push in a small model. Run the other four conditions on seed 0, as the paper's Reproduce runs `pythia_warmup`: `pythia_parallel`, `pythia_lr`, `pythia_batch` and `pythia_all`. The last two take about 2 hours each on MPS. Then check which change makes the push and the checkpoint test look like Pythia's.
- [ ] Test whether the negative side's small lead over random features on the model's next-token distribution belongs to that side. Both sides read the same h, so weights learned for the positive side could explain the lead (`papers/default-on-poles/README.md`). Compare the negative side of learned weights with the positive side of the same weights, and keep the random-feature null.
- [ ] Measure verdict stability. Rerunning one prompt on the same unit agrees only about half the time (`docs/analyses/prompt-trim-ab.md`), which limits every registry-level claim. Rerun the review of about 30 records 3 times each (same submission, same reviewer model). Report agreement overall and per gate, and find the noisiest gate. The reviewer crossover (`docs/analyses/reviewer-crossover.md`) left 20% of the variance in balanced accuracy (34% in VERIFIED) unexplained by submission or reviewer. Reusing its 30 submissions would split that into same-reviewer noise and interaction. Then decide whether that gate needs more probes, a different threshold, or a majority over repeated reviews. **(protocol)** if the rule changes.
- [ ] Competing hypotheses per unit. Have the proposer write two or three rival hypotheses for a pole. Have the reviewer write probes that tell them apart, as well as probes against each one. Record which hypothesis survives, reported and not gated. Rival hypotheses force probes that separate readings, not only probes that break one claim. They may also explain why a rerun agrees only about half the time (verdict stability, above). The idea comes from Macke, Gedon and Wahl, "Teaching AI to build scientific models" (`docs/literature/queue.md`). They argue for a range of plausible models, not one best fit. **(protocol)** if the surviving hypothesis ever decides the verdict.
- [ ] Reduce the variance of prompt A/B tests. In `docs/analyses/prompt-trim-ab.md` the same prompt run twice gave an SD of about 0.14 in paired Δ balanced accuracy. So detecting a 0.05 shift needs about 61 units. Most of the noise comes from what each proposer claims, then from how hard each reviewer pushes. Ways to shrink it, roughly cheapest first:
  - *Hold one side fixed.* For a reviewer-prompt change, reuse frozen hypotheses in both arms. For a proposer-prompt change, score both arms' hypotheses on one shared, pooled probe set. Each arm then varies only what it tests.
  - *Split the variance.* Use the stability item above to measure proposer and reviewer variance separately, so we know which side to replicate.
  - *Replicate the noisy side.* Average k reviews (or k proposals) per unit: if the runs are independent, the SD falls by about √k, which is cheaper than adding units.
  - *Compare a continuous outcome.* Use balanced accuracy and distance to each gate's threshold, not the verdict, which flips on small changes near a gate.
  - *Adjust for the unit.* Use each unit's earlier balanced accuracy as a covariate (CUPED-style regression adjustment). Units differ much more than arms do.
  - *Pin what can be pinned.* Use a fixed seed or a low temperature where the provider allows, and fix the probe count, since reviewers overshoot it.
- [ ] Monitor reviewer drift over time. Providers update models behind the same name, so a reviewer can get stricter or more lenient with no change in this repo. Once a month (scheduled workflow or loop), re-review a fixed benchmark of frozen submissions. Include known-bad hypotheses that must be REJECTED and known-good ones that must be VERIFIED. Alert when verdict rates or balanced accuracy move beyond the run-to-run noise measured by the stability item above. Record the provider's reported model ID with each run.
- [ ] Measure which reviewers catch false hypotheses. The swapped units in the null calibration (`docs/analyses/null-calibration.md`) are hypotheses known to be false. Score each reviewer model by how often its probes reject them. Then leave each reviewer out and measure what the registry loses: the false hypotheses that only that reviewer's probes catch. The reviewer crossover (`docs/analyses/reviewer-crossover.md`) shows that the reviewer explains 6–8% of the variance in verdicts, but not which reviewer adds what. The literature page `docs/literature/training-llms-to-predict-world-events.md` suggests this and notes that no item covers it.

- [ ] Open question: how does a CONTESTED unit become uncontested? Under the standing rule (`docs/PROTOCOL.md` §3 step 9, `builder/standing.py`), one VERIFIED reading against any non-VERIFIED one makes the unit CONTESTED. More reviews of the same submission can't settle it: five VERIFIED and one REJECTED is still CONTESTED. The only ways out are side effects. A revision can supersede the submission, a re-measurement can flip one probe set's reading, or an independent review can outweigh self-reviews. The planner doesn't help either. It draws follow-ups from a single record's verdict (`planner.py`), not from the unit's standing. So contested units get no draw of their own.
  - Decide the resolution rule. It could be a majority or pooled-evidence rule over independent readings (e.g. pool the probe sets and recompute P(useful)). It could be a tie-breaking review by a third reviewer model, or "contested means the claim is too broad, so revise it". **(protocol)** if the rule changes.
  - Add a `resolve` (or `adjudicate`) draw kind to `openinterp next` for CONTESTED units that does what the rule says, weighted above `replicate`. Show on the unit page what would settle it.

## Analyses per experiment

Methods from the wider interpretability literature that the registry doesn't use yet. Each one starts as a recorded number, reported and not gated, as in `docs/PROTOCOL.md` §4 "What is not evidence (yet)". It becomes a gate only through a protocol change. The tagging items below (language, WordNet, part of speech) plug into the scan's annotation layer (`harness/annotations.py`). A tagger returns labelled character spans of a document. The scan aligns them to tokens and reports each label set's R² and mutual information.

- [ ] Investigate using part-of-speech (PoS) tags in the process. Tag the scan corpus (e.g. spaCy, or Universal Dependencies' gold tags, which `openinterp concepts --dataset ud-upos` writes as spans). Align word tags to model tokens, and report the PoS distribution of the pole's top activations against the corpus base rate. Uses to try:
  - *proposer*: give it the PoS enrichment as evidence, so syntactic neurons ("verbs after a modal") are easier to spot than from raw tokens.
  - *reviewer*: build hard negatives from the same word in a different role ("run" as noun vs verb) and check probe labels against the tags.
  - *triage*: flag poles whose activations are explained by PoS alone, as a cheap prior alongside `neuron-family-triage`.
- [ ] Grammatical number as a labelled concept for `sparse-probing-search`. Universal Dependencies' gold morphology labels each noun and verb as singular or plural. So `openinterp concepts` can write number spans, as it does for `ud-upos`. Find the neurons that encode plural subjects and audit them. Then test them causally: ablate the neuron on a plural subject, and check that the next verb changes from plural to singular. Solinsky and Pakhomov, "Encoding and Propagation of Grammatical Number in Large Language Models" (arXiv 2611.07283, `docs/literature/queue.md`), find such features with SAEs on Pythia-70M. Their plural features have more effect than their singular ones. Check whether native neurons show the same asymmetry.

  Check first how often subword splits and tagger errors make the alignment unreliable.
- [ ] Replicate a neuron-basis circuit on a registry model. Arora, Wu, Schwettmann and Steinhardt (arXiv 2601.22594, `docs/literature/queue.md`) report that MLP neurons are about as sparse a basis as SAE features. They also find that a circuit of about 100 neurons controls subject-verb agreement. This is direct outside support for auditing native neurons. Trace that circuit on Qwen2.5-0.5B or GPT-2 with their method. Then report how many of its neurons the registry has studied and how many are VERIFIED. Check whether those hypotheses mention grammatical number. The circuit also gives the grammatical-number item (above) a ground truth.
- [ ] Test a weight-only monosemanticity signal as a cheap prior. ROTATE (arXiv 2604.06005, `docs/literature/queue.md`) flags neurons that encode one concept from the kurtosis of their vocabulary projections, with no forward passes. Compute it for every neuron in a few registry layers. Then check whether it predicts VERIFIED among studied poles. Use a held-out split, as in *Predict whether an unstudied neuron will verify*. If it does, add it to `neuron-family-triage` and to that predictor's features.
- [ ] Add spaCy named-entity recognition as a tagger in the scan's annotation layer (`harness/annotations.py`). Each entity becomes a span labelled with its type (PERSON, ORG, GPE, DATE, MONEY, …). The scan reports the R² and mutual information of the entity labels. That surfaces entity neurons ("fires on city names") as evidence for the proposer. It gives the reviewer same-word hard negatives across entity types ("Paris" the city vs Paris Hilton). It lets triage spot poles explained by entity type alone. spaCy also does the PoS item above, so share one parse per document. Put spaCy and its model in an optional dependency group, and check the model's licence. Measure how much it slows the corpus scan (`corpus.jsonl` already takes ~13 min on CPU).
- [ ] Adversarial input search, the text analogue of feature visualisation. Optimise prompts to maximise, or exactly hit, the pole's activation, using GCG/EPO-style fluent optimisation (Thompson et al., "Fluent dreaming", 2024). Check the hypothesis on the results. This automated reviewer arm hunts for false positives and negatives an LLM reviewer wouldn't think of. Record how many optimised inputs the hypothesis mispredicts.
- [ ] Causal-abstraction check (Geiger et al., 2024, `stanfordnlp/pyvene`). Take a hypothesis that names a variable ("inside a quotation"). Compare the interchange-intervention accuracy of swapping the pole between counterfactual pairs with that of a learned one-dimensional DAS direction for the same variable. Probing is correlational, and this is its causal counterpart. A neuron close to the DAS ceiling is the model's variable. One far below it is a partial view of it.
- [ ] Report dependence in information units (bits) next to correlations. Taleb's "entropy maps" prompted this ([post](https://x.com/nntaleb/status/2115611559594787063), 2026-10-29). The post says "correlation and covariances do not reflect informational distances". The maps are from Taleb, Zalloua, Elbassioni, Henschel and Platt, "Informational rescaling of PCA maps with application to genetic distance" ([arXiv:2303.12654](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12654), *Comput. Struct. Biotechnol. J.* 27:48–56, 2024). Each PCA loading ρ (the correlation between a variable and a component) is replaced by the Gaussian mutual information with its sign kept, −sgn(ρ)·½·log(1−ρ²). Order along each axis is preserved. Weak correlations shrink towards the centre and strong ones stretch outward (ρ = 0.5 carries about 0.14 nats, ρ = 0.7 about 0.34). The paper assumes jointly Gaussian variables and says itself that this fails for heavy tails, where ρ = 0 doesn't mean independence.
  - Our activations are sparse and heavy-tailed, so don't apply the formula to Pearson ρ. Use a Gaussian-copula estimate instead: rank-transform each variable to normal scores, then −½·log(1−ρ²) (Ince et al., 2017). It is a lower bound on the mutual information and does not depend on the marginals. Or use a binned estimate that treats the spike at zero as its own bin.
  - *Partner neurons* (`harness/covariance.py`): rank partners by co-activation lift with Spearman and Pearson for reference. Add mutual information between poles in bits. It is additive and also catches non-monotonic dependence.
  - *Confounds* (`harness/confounds.py`): next to `token_r2` and `position_r2` (variance explained), report the mutual information between activation and token or position, in bits. Label sets already get a binned, Miller–Madow-corrected estimate (`harness/annotations.py`). Token identity has tens of thousands of levels, so check its bias before reusing it.
  - The paper's map itself doesn't carry over: the concept map (Site) plans UMAP over text embeddings, not PCA loadings. Revisit only if a layout is ever built from correlations between neurons.
- [ ] Multi-dimensional features (Engels et al., 2024). Check whether the pole and its partner poles together trace a circular or ordinal manifold, such as days of the week, months or small numbers. Fit PCA to their joint activations on the activating set. Allow hypotheses of the form "one coordinate of a k-dimensional feature" so these neurons aren't forced into a single-concept story and rejected.
- [ ] Record the studied model's per-token surprisal in the scan, and report how well activation correlates with it (reported, not gated). That flags entropy and token-frequency neurons (`neuron-family-triage`) and hypotheses that amount to "fires on unexpected tokens". Split out of the LLMLingua item (`docs/analyses/prompt-compression.md`).
  - The neuron scan now stops at its layer (`runner.layer_activations` in `layer_chunks`, `harness/scan.py`), as the head scan does (`head_outputs`). So surprisal costs the rest of the forward pass and the logits. On Qwen2.5 (151,936 tokens) they take about 1.2 GB per 2,048-token batch, which was most of a scan's memory. Compute it on a smaller batch, or one window at a time, and keep only the surprisal. A replayed scan has no logits: the layer cache has to store each token's surprisal too, and older cache entries count as misses. Check what the feature scan's `dictionary_io` runs.
  - Store it in `ConfoundProfile` as an optional field, so older signed records still validate. A streaming Pearson correlation fits the accumulator as it is. Spearman would need the per-token values kept.
  - Never show it to judges, who must apply the explanation literally (`openinterp score`). An unexplained cue would leak into the score.
- [ ] Should we look at other text classifiers to inform our tagging?

## Performance

Make each experiment faster and cheaper in tokens without changing what it measures. Baseline from the 2026-10-20 loop (claim commit to result commit in git history):

- **Time:** an explore draw takes 25–40 min, and a replicate that skips the scan about 5 min. The CPU corpus scan is roughly 13–15 min of an explore draw.
- **Prompt sizes:** proposer prompts (`ev.prompt.md`) are 57–62 KB, about 15k tokens. Reviewer prompts are about 30 KB, and the scan evidence (`ev.json`) about 500 KB.

Measure before optimising, and check that any change to what the agents see doesn't move verdict rates. Verdicts are noisy: rerunning one prompt on the same unit agrees only about half the time. So an A/B needs about 16 units to detect a 0.10 shift in balanced accuracy (`docs/analyses/prompt-trim-ab.md`).

- [ ] Make the sister search fast, so it stays part of the release (`release` skill, step 3) without a release waiting hours on it. The 0.0.12 run (2026-11-02) took over two hours for five pairs. Its last pair was GPT-2 → Qwen2.5-1.5B: 74 poles, and a first search because Qwen2.5-1.5B was newly configured. It swapped on a 64 GB Mac and was still unfinished after another two. Today each ordered pair with any new VERIFIED pole runs both models over all of `corpus.jsonl` twice (forward, then backward). So cost scales with the number of pairs (n² in models), not poles: 2 poles cost as much as 74. Three models and six pairs is 24 corpus passes. In rough order of payoff:
  - **One pass per model, not per pair.** A pole's own series is tiny (one number per token, ~212k). Compute every new pole's series once. Then run each model once, correlating its layers with every incoming series from all other models at once. Align tokenizers on the CPU from character offsets. Run a second pass per model for the backward partners and rotations. That's 2 passes per model (6, not 24, for three models), whatever the number of models. Rows must come out identical to today's (pin with a test on a small corpus).
  - **Cut peak memory.** `run_with_cache` clones every requested layer at full `[batch, seq, width]` and `_gather` then keeps only the boundary rows. Gather them inside the hook. `_backward` holds a `[width, width]` rotation per matched layer on the device at once. That is 8960² floats ≈ 320 MB each, up to ~9 GB for Qwen2.5-1.5B. Process them a few layers at a time. Empty the MPS cache between batches. The 0.0.12 run held 27 GB through the forward pass and 50 GB in the backward pass. Swapping slowed it from ~850 to ~365 positions a minute.
  - **Free memory between pairs.** The 0.0.17 run (2026-11-11) grew to 57 GB in one process. Its last pair, GPT-2 → Qwen2.5-1.5B, stalled in swap after 20k of 193k forward positions. A fresh process ran that pair alone at a peak of 36 GB, at about 200 positions a second. So something from the earlier pairs stays allocated. Release each pair's models, caches and tensors and empty the MPS cache before the next pair, or run each pair in its own process.
  - **Stop the forward pass at the deepest layer needed** on the side whose poles are fixed.
  - **Try bf16/fp16 on MPS**, checking that the correlations and which matches are sisters don't move. On one 2,048-token batch, fp16 MPS activations were within 1% of fp32 and at most 1.25× faster (`docs/analyses/ane-feasibility.md`).

  Don't subsample the corpus: rows over a different sample aren't comparable with the existing ones. Target: a run with no new model in ~15–25 min on an M-series Mac. This is an estimate, so measure it.

## Later stage

Research directions to pick up once the registry covers several models well.

- [ ] A registry coverage metric, as a concrete form of complete explainability of each token. Mean-ablate every neuron in a layer that isn't VERIFIED, or replace each verified pole with its hypothesis-simulated activation, and measure the share of loss recovered. This is a running measure of how much of the model the registry explains.
- [ ] Predict whether an unstudied neuron will verify, from cheap features: family triage (`neuron-family-triage`), the confounds (`token_r2`, `position_r2`), output norm, activation sparsity and layer. Train on the registry's verdicts (1,289 records on 2026-10-30) with a held-out split, and report how well it ranks. Hold out later records and whole units, not random rows. Protocol versions, reviewers and corpora change over the registry's life, and many records share a unit. Score it with a proper scoring rule (Brier or log score) against the base rate of VERIFIED in earlier records, not against 50%. If it beats that base rate, let the planner use it to steer explore draws. Keep some draws uniform so the registry's statistics stay unbiased.
- [ ] Mistral Large 4 ("Le Chonk", [announcement](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-large-4-oss/), `mistralai/Mistral-Large-4-1T-A52B`): about 1T parameters. It is a mixture-of-experts (about 52B active, going by the repo name) with multimodal input and one hybrid instruct-and-reasoning checkpoint (no base model). Open weights under the "Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight license" (modified MIT). Model-as-a-Service use needs a separate commercial licence, so check it covers publishing activations and excerpts). Far beyond the CPU harness: it needs multi-GPU or a hosted runtime. Its config is now published. It has 72 layers of width 6,144. The first is dense, and each other has 128 routed experts of 6,144 neurons (4 per token) and one shared expert. The weights are stored in FP8, about 14.5 GB of experts per layer. transformers 5.20.1 has no `mistral_large4` model type yet. Depends on MoE support in Models. Streaming reads only its routed experts (`docs/moe.md`, section 8).
- [ ] Factual recall and editing. Use causal tracing (Meng et al., 2022) to check whether verified neurons sit on the fact-recall path at the subject's last token. Then investigate inserting and removing memories or behaviours. Keep in mind the critiques of the knowledge-neuron view (Niu et al., 2024).
- [ ] Investigate model concept merging or diffing. Start with base ↔ instruct pairs: which verified neurons keep their hypothesis after instruction tuning, and which change. Crosscoders (Lindsey et al., 2024) are the learned-basis comparison.
- [ ] Developmental tracking: follow VERIFIED neurons through Pythia and OLMo training checkpoints. When does the pole appear, and does its hypothesis hold from then on? Does its birth line up with loss plateaus or with changes in the local learning coefficient (Lau et al., 2023, the `devinterp` library)?
- [ ] Training-data links: on OLMo (open data), count a verified neuron's trigger n-grams and contexts in the pretraining corpus (e.g. with infini-gram). Test whether concept frequency predicts which neurons become clean. Gradient-based attribution (TracIn, EK-FAC influence functions, Grosse et al., 2023) is only affordable on Pythia-70M-scale models.
- [ ] Standard benchmarks once attention heads land: MIB (Mueller et al., 2025) for circuit localisation. Then RAVEL (Huang et al., 2024) for whether verified neurons separate an entity's attributes (a city's country from its continent).
- [ ] The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH), once multiple models are well understood. Then try snap training with Procrustes: fit an orthogonal Procrustes map between two models' activations on shared text. Then train one model to snap onto the other's representation. Check whether VERIFIED neurons map to their counterparts and keep their hypotheses after the snap.
- [ ] Investigate simplifying neural pathways.
- [ ] Weight space programming.
- [ ] Eventually, extend the registry beyond text language models: image models (e.g. ViTs, CLIP), audio models (e.g. Whisper), video models, JEPA world models (e.g. V-JEPA) and weather models (e.g. GraphCast, Aurora). Each needs its own scan input in place of the token corpus (images, audio clips, video frames, weather fields). It needs a unit identifier that fits its architecture. It also needs a way to show top-activating examples to the proposer and reviewer. Those could be image patches and frames, spectrograms or clips, and map regions, rather than highlighted tokens. Decide too what a probe is when the reviewer can't write text inputs. Start with one image model, and check its MLP layers against the hook resolver (`harness/hooks.py`) first.
