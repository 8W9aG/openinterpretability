## Protocol and science

- [ ] Report registry-wide statistics (share VERIFIED, share dead, share default-on) over `selection = "random"` records only, with the unauditable draws in `wiki/draws/` as outcomes. The other items in this section assume these numbers exist.
- [ ] Measure verdict stability. Rerunning one prompt on the same unit agrees only about half the time (`docs/analyses/prompt-trim-ab.md`), which limits every registry-level claim. Rerun the review of about 30 records 3 times each (same submission, same reviewer model), report agreement overall and per gate, and find the noisiest gate. The reviewer crossover (`docs/analyses/reviewer-crossover.md`) left 20% of the variance in balanced accuracy (34% in VERIFIED) unexplained by submission or reviewer; reusing its 30 submissions would split that into same-reviewer noise and interaction. Then decide whether that gate needs more probes, a different threshold, or a majority over repeated reviews. **(protocol)** if the rule changes.
- [ ] Reduce the variance of prompt A/B tests. In `docs/analyses/prompt-trim-ab.md` the same prompt run twice gave an SD of about 0.14 in paired Δ balanced accuracy, so detecting a 0.05 shift needs about 61 units. Most of the noise comes from what each proposer claims, then from how hard each reviewer pushes. Ways to shrink it, roughly cheapest first:
  - *Hold one side fixed.* For a reviewer-prompt change, reuse frozen hypotheses in both arms. For a proposer-prompt change, score both arms' hypotheses on one shared, pooled probe set. Each arm then varies only what it tests.
  - *Split the variance.* Use the stability item above to measure proposer and reviewer variance separately, so we know which side to replicate.
  - *Replicate the noisy side.* Average k reviews (or k proposals) per unit: if the runs are independent, the SD falls by about √k, which is cheaper than adding units.
  - *Compare a continuous outcome.* Use balanced accuracy and distance to each gate's threshold, not the verdict, which flips on small changes near a gate.
  - *Adjust for the unit.* Use each unit's earlier balanced accuracy as a covariate (CUPED-style regression adjustment). Units differ much more than arms do.
  - *Pin what can be pinned.* Use a fixed seed or a low temperature where the provider allows, and fix the probe count, since reviewers overshoot it.
- [ ] Monitor reviewer drift over time. Providers update models behind the same name, so a reviewer can get stricter or more lenient with no change in this repo. Once a month (scheduled workflow or loop), re-review a fixed benchmark of frozen submissions, including known-bad hypotheses that must be REJECTED and known-good ones that must be VERIFIED, and alert when verdict rates or balanced accuracy move beyond the run-to-run noise measured by the stability item above. Record the provider's reported model ID with each run.
- [ ] Ground-truth calibration: audit models whose mechanisms are known by construction, and check that agents recover each neuron's role. Candidates: Tracr-compiled transformers, InterpBench (Gupta et al., 2024), and a small model fine-tuned to plant a known neuron. This complements the induction-head positive control: that control checks the protocol can say yes, while this checks that the agent's explanation is the *right* one.
- [ ] **(protocol)** Decide whether steering predictions and simulation scores should become gates. Both need a pre-registered prediction format first, and simulation scores need the backfill (Pipeline) to show whether VERIFIED predicts held-out detection.
- [ ] **(protocol)** Hypothesis-conditioned resample ablation, i.e. causal scrubbing for one neuron (Chan et al., 2022). Replace the pole's activation with its value on another token the hypothesis labels the same, and separately with its value on a token labelled differently. If the hypothesis captures what downstream layers use, the first barely changes the loss and the second does. This needs the hypothesis as an executable labeller (the simulator in `explanation-simulation-scoring`). ## Analyses per experiment

Methods from the wider interpretability literature that the registry doesn't use yet. Each one starts as a recorded number (reported, not gated, as in `docs/PROTOCOL.md` §4 "What is not evidence (yet)") and becomes a gate only through a protocol change. The tagging items below (language, WordNet, syntax trees, part of speech) plug into the scan's annotation layer (`harness/annotations.py`): a tagger returns labelled character spans of a document, and the scan aligns them to tokens and reports each label set's R² and mutual information.

- [ ] Use WordNet (via `nltk.corpus.wordnet` or the `wn` package; permissive licence) for lexical-semantic structure: synsets, hypernyms ("Paris" → city → municipality), hyponyms, siblings and word senses. Uses, each reported and not gated:
  - *proposer*: group a pole's top-activating words by their lowest shared hypernym ("11 of 15 are body parts"), as a candidate concept and its level of generality, and show which words fall outside it;
  - *reviewer*: positive holdouts from other hyponyms of the claimed category that never appear in the top examples, hard negatives from sibling categories (other kinds of places for "cities"), and same-word-other-sense negatives ("bank" of a river against a bank that lends) for the polysemy illusion (`interpretability-illusions-review`);
  - *specificity check*: mean activation over every hyponym of the claimed synset against its siblings, a cheap test of whether a "cities" neuron is really a "places" neuron.

  Limits: English only (the Open Multilingual Wordnet covers other languages more thinly); it knows words, not subword tokens, so it needs the same word alignment as the PoS item below; coverage of names, slang, code and recent terms is poor; and labelling senses in running text needs word-sense disambiguation, which is unreliable, so use senses for writing probes rather than for labelling the corpus.
- [ ] Parse code in the scan corpus into syntax trees (e.g. [tree-sitter](https://tree-sitter.github.io), which covers many languages and tolerates broken or truncated code) and give each token its syntactic context: node type (identifier, keyword, string, comment, number, operator), bracket and indentation depth, and the enclosing construct (function definition, call arguments, import). This is the code analogue of the PoS item below, and an easier one: the parser gives character offsets, which map onto the tokenizer's offsets exactly. Uses, each reported and not gated:
  - *confounds and triage*: how much of a pole's activation the node type and nesting depth explain, which flags syntax neurons (bracket depth, inside a string, indentation) before anyone writes a hypothesis;
  - *proposer*: the syntactic context of each top example in the evidence;
  - *reviewer*: hard negatives that keep the token but change its role (`return` as a keyword, inside a string, inside a comment), and positive holdouts in another programming language for hypotheses that claim to be language-independent;
  - *positive controls*: bracket-matching and indentation neurons are known to exist in code models, so a syntax-labelled corpus can check that the pipeline finds them.

  It needs code to scan: pile-10k has some GitHub text, mixed in, and no preset is code-only. Add a code corpus preset first (Datasets; check the licence and gating, e.g. of `bigcode/the-stack-smol`), and tag each document's programming language there, as `lang` does for natural languages.
- [ ] Open question: can we detect programming languages in unlabelled corpora? pile-10k mixes GitHub text into prose, and no field marks it. A per-document detector (e.g. Google's Magika, guesslang, or GitHub Linguist's heuristics) would tag code and its language without a code-only preset, for the syntax-tree item above and for the reviewer and site uses language labels already have. As with natural languages (`docs/analyses/language-detection.md`), check accuracy on short and mixed documents on a labelled sample first. fastText's `lid.176` already leaves most code unlabelled (below its 0.8 line), so a code detector could label what it leaves.
- [ ] Investigate using part-of-speech (PoS) tags in the process. Tag the scan corpus (e.g. spaCy, or Universal Dependencies' gold tags, which `openinterp concepts --dataset ud-upos` writes as spans), align word tags to model tokens, and report the PoS distribution of the pole's top activations against the corpus base rate. Uses to try:
  - *proposer*: give it the PoS enrichment as evidence, so syntactic neurons ("verbs after a modal") are easier to spot than from raw tokens;
  - *reviewer*: build hard negatives from the same word in a different role ("run" as noun vs verb) and check probe labels against the tags;
  - *triage*: flag poles whose activations are explained by PoS alone, as a cheap prior alongside `neuron-family-triage`.

  Check first how often subword splits and tagger errors make the alignment unreliable.
- [ ] Adversarial input search, the text analogue of feature visualisation. Optimise prompts to maximise, or exactly hit, the pole's activation, using GCG/EPO-style fluent optimisation (Thompson et al., "Fluent dreaming", 2024), and check the hypothesis on the results. This automated reviewer arm hunts for false positives and negatives an LLM reviewer wouldn't think of. Record how many optimised inputs the hypothesis mispredicts.
- [ ] Causal-abstraction check (Geiger et al., 2024; `stanfordnlp/pyvene`). For a hypothesis that names a variable ("inside a quotation"), compare the interchange-intervention accuracy of swapping the pole between counterfactual pairs with that of a learned one-dimensional DAS direction for the same variable. Probing is correlational; this is its causal counterpart. A neuron close to the DAS ceiling is the model's variable; one far below it is a partial view of it.
- [ ] Report dependence in information units (bits) next to correlations. Prompted by Taleb's "entropy maps" ([post](https://x.com/nntaleb/status/2115611559594787063), 2026-10-29: "correlation and covariances do not reflect informational distances"), which are from Taleb, Zalloua, Elbassioni, Henschel and Platt, "Informational rescaling of PCA maps with application to genetic distance" ([arXiv:2303.12654](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12654); *Comput. Struct. Biotechnol. J.* 27:48–56, 2024). Each PCA loading ρ (the correlation between a variable and a component) is replaced by the Gaussian mutual information with its sign kept, −sgn(ρ)·½·log(1−ρ²). Order along each axis is preserved, weak correlations shrink towards the centre and strong ones stretch outward (ρ = 0.5 carries about 0.14 nats, ρ = 0.7 about 0.34). The paper assumes jointly Gaussian variables and says itself that this fails for heavy tails, where ρ = 0 doesn't mean independence.
  - Our activations are sparse and heavy-tailed, so don't apply the formula to Pearson ρ. Use a Gaussian-copula estimate instead (rank-transform each variable to normal scores, then −½·log(1−ρ²); Ince et al., 2017), which is a lower bound on the mutual information and robust to the marginals, or a binned estimate that treats the spike at zero as its own bin.
  - *Partner neurons* (`harness/covariance.py`): rank partners by co-activation lift with Spearman and Pearson for reference; add mutual information between poles in bits, which is additive and also catches non-monotonic dependence.
  - *Confounds* (`harness/confounds.py`): next to `token_r2` and `position_r2` (variance explained), report the mutual information between activation and token or position, in bits. Label sets already get a binned, Miller–Madow-corrected estimate (`harness/annotations.py`); token identity has tens of thousands of levels, so check its bias before reusing it.
  - The paper's map itself doesn't carry over: the concept map (Site) plans UMAP over text embeddings, not PCA loadings. Revisit only if a layout is ever built from correlations between neurons.
- [ ] Multi-dimensional features (Engels et al., 2024): check whether the pole and its partner poles together trace a circular or ordinal manifold, such as days of the week, months or small numbers. Fit PCA to their joint activations on the activating set. Allow hypotheses of the form "one coordinate of a k-dimensional feature" so these neurons aren't forced into a single-concept story and rejected.
- [ ] Record the studied model's per-token surprisal in the scan, and report how well activation correlates with it (reported, not gated). That flags entropy and token-frequency neurons (`neuron-family-triage`) and hypotheses that amount to "fires on unexpected tokens". Split out of the LLMLingua item (`docs/analyses/prompt-compression.md`).
  - The neuron scan already computes the logits and throws them away (`_, cache = runner.run_with_cache(...)` in `layer_chunks`, `harness/scan.py`), so surprisal costs a log-softmax per batch on a fresh scan. A replayed scan has no logits: the layer cache has to store each token's surprisal too, and older cache entries count as misses. The head scan stops at its layer (`head_outputs`), so there it costs the rest of the forward pass; check what the feature scan's `dictionary_io` runs.
  - Store it in `ConfoundProfile` as an optional field, so older signed records still validate. A streaming Pearson correlation fits the accumulator as it is; Spearman would need the per-token values kept.
  - Never show it to judges, who must apply the explanation literally (`openinterp score`); an unexplained cue would leak into the score.

## Performance

Make each experiment faster and cheaper in tokens without changing what it measures. Baseline from the 2026-10-20 loop (claim commit to result commit in git history):

- **Time:** an explore draw takes 25–40 min, and a replicate that skips the scan about 5 min. The CPU corpus scan is roughly 13–15 min of an explore draw.
- **Prompt sizes:** proposer prompts (`ev.prompt.md`) are 57–62 KB, about 15k tokens. Reviewer prompts are about 30 KB, and the scan evidence (`ev.json`) about 500 KB.

Measure before optimising, and check that any change to what the agents see doesn't move verdict rates. Verdicts are noisy: rerunning one prompt on the same unit agrees only about half the time, so an A/B needs about 16 units to detect a 0.10 shift in balanced accuracy (`docs/analyses/prompt-trim-ab.md`).

- [ ] Order prompts for provider prompt caching. Put the stable parts (system prompt, methodology, answer schema) first and the neuron's evidence last, and pass cache-control hints through LiteLLM, so repeated experiments reuse the cached prefix.
- [ ] Keep per-experiment checks proportional to the change. `openinterp check` and the wiki index rebuild walk every record, so their cost grows with the registry. Check only the records a branch adds or changes (`--base-ref origin/main`, as `reproduce` does), and keep the full check for CI on `main`.
- [ ] Skip lint and tests on experiment PRs. When every changed file is under `wiki/` (the same rule as "Auto-merge experiment-only PRs" under Merge verification), the code is the same as on `main`, so CI's "Lint and tests" job and the skills' local `make check lint test` re-test code the PR didn't change.
  - In `.github/workflows/ci.yml`, add a job that lists the changed files, as `record-changes` does, and gate `tests` on it with a job-level `if`. Don't use `paths-ignore` on the workflow: a required check that never starts blocks the merge, while a skipped job counts as passed. Keep running it on pushes to `main`.
  - In `choose-next-experiment` (step 7) and `land-experiment`, run `make check` only, and keep `lint test` for branches that change code.
- [ ] Find out how to run in low-memory environments (e.g. 8 GB laptops, free Colab, small CI runners). First measure peak RAM/VRAM for `scan`, `audit` and `review` on each registry model, then try what lowers it, roughly in this order:
  - Load in bf16/fp16 rather than fp32.
  - Keep only the target layer's activations rather than every layer's.
  - Use smaller batches and stream the corpus rather than loading it all.
  - Offload layers to CPU or disk (`accelerate`'s `device_map="auto"`).
  - Quantise to 8- or 4-bit loading.

  Lower precision and quantisation change activations, so check that records made this way still reproduce (records already pin their dtype, which `openinterp/reproduce.py` checks; quantisation would need pinning too). Document the minimum memory per model in the README.

Running several loops on one machine is under Pipeline.

## Merge verification

CI checks each pull request in two ways. "Experiment records" (`openinterp check`) confirms that the PR adds a valid record and its wiki page. "Reproduce records" (`openinterp reproduce --base-ref origin/main`, docs/PROTOCOL.md §5) re-runs every added or changed record on GitHub-hosted CPU runners, with cached weights and a model allow-list, so a record with edited activations or a hand-written verdict fails. Reproductions run one at a time across all pull requests, in a queue. That is enough while volume is low. The items below are for when it isn't: larger models, many PRs, or runner minutes adding up.

- [ ] Auto-merge experiment-only PRs: when every changed file is under `wiki/` (experiment records, their `.sig` files, pages and generated indexes) and "Experiment records" and "Reproduce records" pass (reproduced, or signed by an allowed signer), merge the PR automatically. Any change outside `wiki/` (code, workflows, `.github/allowed_signers`) keeps the PR manual. Make those checks required first, so GitHub's auto-merge waits for them. Fork PRs need a separate `pull_request_target` workflow that only reads the PR's file list through the API and enables auto-merge, and never checks out or runs the PR's code with a write token. Squash-merge so `main` gets one commit per experiment.
- [ ] Re-queue automatically when a PR's record changes, and let maintainers re-run or skip a job by hand (e.g. a label) for records whose model the worker can't load yet.
- [ ] Re-reproduce old records on a schedule. CI reproduces only the records a PR changes, so a torch or transformers upgrade that silently shifts activations goes unnoticed. Add a nightly job that runs `openinterp reproduce` on a rotating sample of signed records (seeded by date, so over time every record is covered) and opens an issue on a mismatch.
- [ ] Harden agent prompts against prompt injection. Corpus text, submitted hypotheses and probe text go into proposer, reviewer and judge prompts verbatim, so a crafted document or a fork PR could tell the reviewer to go easy. Fence untrusted text with unambiguous delimiters, tell each agent that fenced text is data and never instructions, and add a test with a planted instruction in a corpus document that must not change the verdict.
- [ ] Plan signing-key rotation and revocation. `.github/allowed_signers` lists keys with no expiry, and nothing says what to do if a key leaks. Use `valid-after`/`valid-before` options per key, document rotation in `SECURITY.md`, and add `openinterp check --resign-window` (or similar) to find records signed by a revoked key in its exposure window so CI reproduces them instead of trusting the signature.

- [ ] Feasibility study: a concept map on model pages, in the style of "2.5 million cancer research papers, mapped" (embed each item's text → k-NN graph → UMAP → Leiden clusters, labelled by theme). Each point is a neuron, placed by the meaning of the text it fires on, not by its activations or weights. Write up the findings and a recommendation before building anything.
  - **The embedding.** Use a text embedding (sentence-embedding model or embeddings API) of the text each pole fires on: its top-activating contexts, with the peak token marked inside a window of about ±20 tokens. A neuron's vector is the mean embedding of its positive-pole texts minus the mean embedding of its negative-pole texts. Then L2-normalise it and compare with cosine. So a "Paris as a city" neuron lands near other city neurons. Two neurons whose poles swap the same concepts (+city/−code and +code/−city) point opposite ways. Text that both poles share, such as "this is English web text", cancels out. Things to settle:
    - *Weighting.* Use a plain mean, or weight each text by its activation or rank so the strongest examples count most. Means keep the two poles on the same scale even when they keep different numbers of examples.
    - *Near-zero vectors.* When both poles fire on similar text, the difference is mostly noise. Record the pre-normalisation norm as a confidence, and drop or fade neurons below a threshold.
    - *One meaningful pole.* When one pole is scanned but dead or diffuse, the vector is the live pole's concept minus generic text. Compare that with treating the weak pole as missing (zero).
    - *Two unrelated poles.* A neuron with +Paris and −Python code sits on the direction between the two. Measure how often this happens, using studied neurons whose explanations cover both poles, and decide whether those neurons need per-pole points as well.
  - **Missing poles count as zero.** A pole with no scanned texts contributes a zero vector, so the neuron's vector is the mean embedding of the pole it does have, negated if that pole is the negative one. This means the existing records can draw a map now, with no re-scan. There are about 100 of them against 116,736 neurons in Qwen2.5-0.5B (24 × 4,864), and most cover one pole only. Two caveats:
    - In a two-pole neuron, text the poles share cancels out. In a one-pole neuron it doesn't, so one-pole neurons all carry the same generic "web text" component and may clump together, or sit apart from two-pole neurons that have the same concept. Test subtracting the corpus-mean embedding from each pole mean, or measure how much the clumping matters.
    - A neuron known only by its negative pole points away from its concept. That's consistent with the two-pole rule (−city sits opposite +city), but the map should show which neurons are one-pole.

    Growing coverage beyond the records still needs top-k contexts for more neurons. That means one pass over the corpus with hooks on every layer, keeping a top-k heap of (snippet, position) per pole. One pole per neuron is now enough to place it, though both are better. The scan already reads whole layers (`CovarianceAccumulator` takes `layer_acts`), so the hooks exist, and the forward pass costs roughly one scan. Embedding is up to 233k × k short texts; cost and time it for a local model and for an API.
  - **Check it against explanations.** For studied neurons, embed `natural_language_explanation` too and compare it with the context-difference vector, e.g. how often the two are nearest neighbours. Also check that neurons with similar VERIFIED explanations land together, across layers.
  - **Drop neurons with nothing to say first.** Dead neurons (`neuron-family-triage`) are one case. Purely positional or frequency neurons are another: their "concept" is a position, and their contexts would scatter them as noise. Place those in their own labelled regions, or leave them out.
  - **Labelling clusters.** The cancer map names themes. Name clusters from the explanations of their studied members where there are some, otherwise from an LLM summary of the members' positive and negative contexts. That summary must read as unverified, so a cluster name never looks like a VERIFIED claim. Colour studied neurons by status and link them to neuron pages.
  - **Fit with the build.** `umap-learn` (numba, pynndescent, scikit-learn), `leidenalg`/`python-igraph` and any local embedding model are heavy. Put them in an optional dependency group, run the map offline (`openinterp map <model>`), and commit the coordinates, cluster IDs and norms (a few MB per model), so the site build stays light and deterministic. UMAP is only reproducible with `random_state` set, which makes it single-threaded; time that on 117k points. 117k SVG circles would be several MB, which breaks the static-SVG rule above. Render the density as a build-time PNG and draw the studied neurons as an SVG overlay.
  - **Questions to answer.** Does the map show coherent semantic clusters? Do the neurons for one concept at different layers (e.g. every "city" neuron) sit together? Is the map stable across seeds, choices of k and embedding models? Can the planner use it to draw from unexplored or unexplained clusters? Say plainly in the write-up that distances between clusters on a UMAP don't mean much.

## Models

The hooks already resolve Llama/Qwen2/Mistral/Gemma/Phi, GPT-2, GPT-J, GPT-NeoX and OPT layouts (`openinterp/harness/hooks.py`). Each new model needs a smoke test, a note on its MLP activation (gated SwiGLU/GeGLU vs plain GELU, which changes what a "negative pole" means) and a check that the final-norm path resolves. Then add it to `[tool.openinterp] models` in `pyproject.toml` and to `ALLOWED_MODELS` in `.github/workflows/ci.yml`, and `openinterp next` starts exploring it.

- [ ] Qwen2.5 family: 1.5B, 3B and the `-Instruct` variants, for base ↔ instruct and size-scaling comparisons with the default model.
- [ ] OpenAI's weight-sparse transformers (Gao et al., 2025, `openai/circuit_sparsity`): trained so that circuits are small and neurons are close to monosemantic by construction. They should have a high VERIFIED rate, which makes them a positive-control model family and a reference point for how clean dense models' native neurons are. Check the architecture against the hook resolver first.
- [ ] GPT-2 medium (GPT-2 small is in): the best-documented model family in the circuits literature; good for the positive-control task.
- [ ] SmolLM2 (135M, 360M, 1.7B): fully open data, cheap enough for CI smoke tests.
- [ ] Pythia suite (70M–1.4B, with training checkpoints and the `-deduped` variants): the standard for tracking a neuron across training.
- [ ] EleutherAI's other models ([site](https://eleuther.ai/)), beyond Pythia (item above): e.g. GPT-Neo, GPT-J and GPT-NeoX-20B, whose MLP layouts the hooks already resolve (GPT-Neo's attention output, `attn.attention.out_proj`, isn't in `ATTN_OUT_PATHS` yet).
- [ ] Qwen3 (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B): the successor architecture; check `q_norm`/`k_norm` don't break hook resolution.
- [ ] Llama 3.2 (1B, 3B) and Llama 3.1 8B (gated licence).
- [ ] Gemma 2 (2B, 9B) and Gemma 3 (1B, 4B): GeGLU MLPs, and Gemma Scope / Gemma Scope 2 SAEs and transcoders for the baseline arm.
- [ ] OLMo 2 (1B, 7B): open data and checkpoints, so activating examples can be traced to training documents.
- [ ] Marin models (Stanford's Marin project with Open Athena; `marin-community/marin-8b-base` and `-instruct` on Hugging Face, newer releases possibly under `open-athena`). Fully open like OLMo: code, data mixes, training logs and intermediate checkpoints, so they also suit tracing activating examples to training documents and developmental tracking (Later stage). Marin 8B uses the Llama 3 8B architecture (32 layers, MLP width 14336, SwiGLU) with a modified Llama 3 tokenizer, so the Llama hook path should resolve; the tokenizer still needs a check. 8B needs a GPU or quantised loading, as Mistral 7B does. ## Later stage

Research directions to pick up once the registry covers several models well.

- [ ] A registry coverage metric, as a concrete form of complete explainability of each token. Mean-ablate every neuron in a layer that isn't VERIFIED, or replace each verified pole with its hypothesis-simulated activation, and measure the share of loss recovered. This is a running measure of how much of the model the registry explains.
- [ ] Predict whether an unstudied neuron will verify, from cheap features: family triage (`neuron-family-triage`), the confounds (`token_r2`, `position_r2`), output norm, activation sparsity and layer. Train on the registry's verdicts (1,289 records on 2026-10-30) with a held-out split, report how well it ranks, and if it beats chance let the planner use it to steer explore draws, keeping some draws uniform so the registry's statistics stay unbiased.
- [ ] Mistral Large 4 ("Le Chonk", [announcement](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-large-4-oss/), `mistralai/Mistral-Large-4-1T-A52B`): about 1T parameters, a mixture-of-experts (about 52B active, going by the repo name) with multimodal input and one hybrid instruct-and-reasoning checkpoint (no base model). Open weights under the "Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight license" (modified MIT; Model-as-a-Service use needs a separate commercial licence, so check it covers publishing activations and excerpts). Far beyond the CPU harness: it needs multi-GPU or a hosted runtime, and expert count, MLP activation and attention type aren't published yet (a technical report is "coming soon"). Depends on the MoE decision in Models.
- [ ] Factual recall and editing: use causal tracing (Meng et al., 2022) to check whether verified neurons sit on the fact-recall path at the subject's last token, then investigate inserting and removing memories or behaviours. Keep in mind the critiques of the knowledge-neuron view (Niu et al., 2024).
- [ ] Investigate model concept merging or diffing. Start with base ↔ instruct pairs: which verified neurons keep their hypothesis after instruction tuning, and which change. Crosscoders (Lindsey et al., 2024) are the learned-basis comparison.
- [ ] Developmental tracking: follow VERIFIED neurons through Pythia and OLMo training checkpoints. When does the pole appear, does its hypothesis hold from then on, and does its birth line up with loss plateaus or with changes in the local learning coefficient (Lau et al., 2023; the `devinterp` library)?
- [ ] Training-data links: on OLMo (open data), count a verified neuron's trigger n-grams and contexts in the pretraining corpus (e.g. with infini-gram) to test whether concept frequency predicts which neurons become clean. Gradient-based attribution (TracIn, EK-FAC influence functions; Grosse et al., 2023) is only affordable on Pythia-70M-scale models.
- [ ] Standard benchmarks once attention heads land: MIB (Mueller et al., 2025) for circuit localisation, and RAVEL (Huang et al., 2024) for whether verified neurons separate an entity's attributes (a city's country from its continent).
- [ ] The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH), once multiple models are well understood. Then try snap training with Procrustes: fit an orthogonal Procrustes map between two models' activations on shared text, then train one model to snap onto the other's representation. Check whether VERIFIED neurons map to their counterparts and keep their hypotheses after the snap.
- [ ] Investigate simplifying neural pathways.
